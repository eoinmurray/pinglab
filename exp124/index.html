Abstract

Our initial one-layer conductance-based networks changed hidden weights and reduced loss, but classification remained weak. We then investigated two-layer training through tiny fitting checks and reduced spoken-digit pilots. Both networks fitted the tiny subset after changing initialization and optimization settings. Held-out accuracy improved but varied across seeds, especially with inhibitory feedback enabled. These checks support a larger learning experiment; they do not establish competitive recognition or a general benefit from inhibition.

Results

One-layer COBA smoke

With feedback disabled, local training loss fell from 3.0018 to 2.9678, and the input-weight matrix changed by 1.219 in its flattened Euclidean norm. Final validation accuracy was 9% in both executions (Fig. 1). Gradients remained finite throughout all updates. CPU and GPU accuracies matched at every measured epoch, while cross-entropy differed slightly. This short run demonstrates learning, while its small validation partition limits conclusions about generalisation.

One-layer PING smoke

With feedback enabled, local training loss fell from 2.9963 to 2.9859, and the input-weight matrix changed by 1.205 in its flattened Euclidean norm. Final validation accuracy was 8% in both executions (Fig. 2). Gradients remained finite throughout all updates. CPU and GPU accuracies matched at every measured epoch, while cross-entropy differed slightly. The two smoke runs are insufficient to determine whether inhibition improves spoken-digit recognition.

One-layer COBA after 20 epochs

In a fresh local run with 1,000 training examples, COBA training loss fell from 3.0022 to 2.886. Final training accuracy was 12.8% and validation accuracy 12.5% (Fig. 3). Hidden and readout weights changed, and gradients remained finite. Low accuracy on both splits indicates limited fitting under this recipe; this single-seed run does not establish competitive recognition.

One-layer PING after 20 epochs

In a fresh local run with 1,000 training examples, PING training loss fell from 2.9963 to 2.885. Final training accuracy was 10.1% and validation accuracy 11% (Fig. 4). Hidden and readout weights changed, and gradients remained finite. Low accuracy on both splits indicates limited fitting under this recipe; this single-seed run does not establish competitive recognition.

Two-layer COBA diagnosis

The selected two-layer COBA recipe fitted 20 training examples to 90% accuracy after 300 optimizer updates (Fig. 5A). On the 200-training/100-validation-example pilots, final validation accuracy was 18% for seed 42 and 14% for seed 43 (Fig. 5D). All three learned matrices changed and received finite, nonzero gradients. These checks establish fitting capacity and modest held-out learning; the small utterance-split pilots remain insufficient to establish competitive performance.

Two-layer PING diagnosis

The selected two-layer PING recipe fitted 20 training examples to 100% accuracy after 100 optimizer updates (Fig. 6A). Final pilot validation accuracy was 44% for seed 42 and 22% for seed 43 (Fig. 6D). All three learned matrices changed and received finite, nonzero gradients. Validation varied substantially despite consistently improved fitting. These two small replicates do not establish a general recognition benefit from inhibition or achievement of the larger-run accuracy target.

Methods

  1. Select independent examples. We used the official training partition of Spiking Heidelberg Digits, which represents spoken English and German digits as events across 700 cochlear-model channels [1]. With seed 42, we selected 15 distinct utterances per class: 10 for training and five for validation. The partitions were disjoint by utterance, not by speaker. All 20 classes remained present. We did not access the official test partition.

  2. Bin spike events. Each presentation lasted one second with a 1 ms integration step. Events were assigned to left-closed time bins and represented as binary channel events. Events at or after one second were discarded. Across the selected examples, 434 events were discarded and no within-channel/time-bin collisions occurred. No stochastic image-to-spikes encoding was applied.

  3. Define matched one-layer networks. Both networks had 128 excitatory and 32 inhibitory conductance-based LIF neurons. Excitatory and inhibitory capacitances were 1 and 0.5 nF; leak conductances were 0.05 and 0.1 μS. Rest/reset voltage was −65 mV, threshold −50 mV, and refractory durations were 2 and 1 ms. AMPA and GABA decay constants were 2 and 6 ms. External spikes targeted excitatory neurons. Fixed reciprocal conductances had summed incoming strengths of 1 μS for E→I and 2 μS for I→E in PING, with a one-step delay; both projections were zero in COBA. No E→E or I→I recurrence was added. The 20 outputs were signed, non-spiking leaky integrators with a 20 ms time constant, scored by mean voltage over the presentation. Hidden and output states reset at every presentation.

  4. Train hidden and readout weights. Both conditions shared learned-weight initialization and minibatch order. Input weights came from a lower-clamped Gaussian with parent mean 0.9 and SD 0.09, divided by the input fan-in. We initially zeroed 95% and compensated surviving weights by the retained fraction; these zeros were not permanently masked. Readout weights came from a Gaussian with mean zero and SD 0.1. We used cross-entropy, AdamW with learning rate 0.0004 and zero weight decay, batches of 16 and five epochs. Fast-sigmoid surrogate gradients had slope 1; voltage-gradient increments were damped by 1000 in both conditions, and the global gradient norm was clipped at 1. Hidden conductance weights remained nonnegative; readout weights were unconstrained. Recurrent weights remained fixed. We retained final-epoch weights without test-based selection.

  5. Measure learning. We evaluated complete training and validation splits before learning and after each completed epoch, weighted loss by the number of examples, and counted correct predictions. We checked finite scores, losses, gradients and final weights, and measured hidden/readout weight changes. These checks establish finite training under this protocol rather than fine-resolution gamma dynamics or official-test performance.

  6. Repeat with more examples and updates. For the larger local run, we used seed 42 to select 60 distinct utterances per class from the official training partition: 50 for training and 10 for validation, giving 1,000 and 200 examples. These partitions were utterance-disjoint, not speaker-disjoint; they were not the smoke’s original split. We initialized both networks afresh and trained for twenty epochs with the same architecture, initialization recipe, optimizer and numerical settings. We discarded 1,842 events at or after one second and recorded no within-channel/time-bin collisions. We evaluated each complete split before training and after every epoch, retained final-epoch weights, and did not access official test data. The original smoke observations in Figures 1–2 are reused; Figures 3–4 reuse the earlier twenty-epoch local measurements.

  7. Stack two hidden layers. For the new diagnostics, we stacked two 128 E/32 I layers with the same neuron and loop settings as the earlier networks. E1 spikes drove E2 through a learned nonnegative AMPA matrix; input→E1 and E2→readout matrices were also learned. Initial interlayer weights had lower-clamped Gaussian parent mean 1.8 and SD 0.18 μS, divided by 128. Sparse variants initially zeroed 90% and compensated survivors by the retained fraction; the mask was not permanent. Learned initialization and example order were matched between COBA and PING within each paired run.

  8. Compare training settings. We used 20 training/20 validation examples for tiny fitting controls, and 200/100 for pilots, balanced across all classes. Displayed tiny controls tested sparse interlayer initialization, damping 100 and fixed score gain 20, then separate hidden/readout learning rates of 0.0004/0.01. Pilot controls compared mean versus maximum voltage, per-example rate normalization, input Gaussian parent mean 0.45 versus 0.9 μS (SD 10% of the mean), and hidden rates 0.001 or 0.002 versus 0.0004. Each presentation still lasted one second at a 1 ms step, with state reset. We evaluated complete splits after each epoch and retained final-epoch metrics rather than the best validation epoch.

  9. Choose and confirm a shared recipe. We selected the shared recipe from seed-42 pilots using joint final validation performance. It used 90% initial interlayer zeros, damping 100, hidden/readout rates 0.0004/0.01, batches of 20 containing one example per class without replacement, and initial readout weights centered across source neurons separately for each class. Mean leaky-integrator voltage was multiplied by gain 20 and by 20 Hz divided by the presentation’s E2 firing rate averaged across its neurons, with a 1 Hz denominator floor. This positive rescaling changes the loss scale while preserving the argmax at fixed weights. Seed-42 validation was used in recipe selection. All partitions remained utterance-disjoint, not speaker-disjoint, and the official test partition remained untouched.

  10. Check the selected fitting protocol. With seed 43, we trained the selected recipe on 20 examples for 100 updates in both models, then ran a separate fresh COBA-only confirmation for 300 updates. The fitting criterion was at least 90% final training accuracy. All three learned matrices were checked for finite, nonzero gradients and changed weights. One tiny epoch was one update; a 200-example pilot epoch was ten. After passing the fitting criterion, we repeated the 200/100-example, twenty-epoch pilot with seed 43, changing initialization and sampled utterances. These controls checked capacity and optimization, while the pilots assessed limited held-out learning. Figures 1–4 reuse one-layer evidence; Figures 5–6 show new two-layer diagnostics.
