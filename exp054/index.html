Abstract

We asked where rhythmic activity appears as reciprocal PING coupling activates across the coupling map. We swept coupling directions in a network and compared autocorrelation structure with private- and shared-input controls.

Rhythmic contrast was weak on uncoupled edges and stronger inside the coupled map; shared input imitated contrast without a recurrent loop. This supports the private-input control, but does not establish rate invariance or the spiking transition’s bifurcation type.

Results

Coupling-map structure

On either zero-coupling edge the loop was broken: E fired at 168.91 Hz and contrast was 0.000301, while I was silent when E-to-I coupling was zero. The diagonal examples in panels D/E/F had contrasts 0.000301/0.268/0.989. Coupled conditions generally showed stronger temporal structure, although the map was not strictly monotonic (Fig. 1).

Coupled-grid rasters

Many coupled-interior conditions showed separated volleys, whereas the zero-E-to-I column had silent I neurons and the zero-I-to-E row lacked inhibitory feedback to E (Fig. 2).

Coupled-grid autocorrelograms

Edge autocorrelograms remained near chance. In the coupled interior, short-lag clustering, suppression between volleys and later recurrence peaks supplied complementary temporal evidence (Fig. 3).

Input-sharing controls

Without an inhibitory loop, the private-input null had a maximum contrast of 0.0392 over 2.00–168.91 Hz. The shared-input null reached 1 at 0.0109 Hz, but that estimate contained only 10 E spikes across the population. At 1.39 Hz its contrast was 0.127. Shared afferents can create short-lag coincidence detected by this score; the sparse extreme also illustrates its finite-sample sensitivity. These single-seed controls do not prove rate invariance or establish rhythmicity from contrast alone (Fig. 4).

Rate-matched null structure

Shared-input examples showed central coincidence without inhibitory feedback; private-input examples showed smaller contrast and finite-sample fluctuations around chance. Their approximate rate matching did not make rates or spike counts equal (Fig. 5).

Methods

Untrained PING populations tested coupling-dependent temporal structure; uncoupled controls tested the influence of input sharing. No external theory or trained-network measurements entered this experiment.

  1. Sweep coupling. We simulated 1,024 E and 256 I neurons at all 11×11 combinations of WEI=0W_{E I} = 0WEI​=0–3 µS and WIE=0W_{I E} = 0WIE​=0–6 µS. Each E neuron received a private 100 Hz Poisson channel with identity weight 0.5. We used a 6 ms GABA decay constant, seed 42, one trial, 0.1 ms steps and 1,000 ms recordings; we discarded the first 100 ms. E/I reset holds were 1.2/0.6 ms.

  2. Construct uncoupled controls. We set both coupling strengths to zero. We scanned private input at 1/2/5/10/20/40/70/100 Hz and shared input at 8/12/16/20/28/40/60/100 Hz. Shared input used 200 channels, weight 0.2 and 95% initial zero connections. The 100 Hz private origin was shared with the coupling grid, giving 136 unique probes.

Supplementary details

Appendix: reading the autocorrelogram

The raw lag product ∑knE[k]nE[k+ℓ]\sum_{k} n_{E [ k ]} n_{E [ k + \ell ]}∑k​nE[k]​nE[k+ℓ]​ counts spike pairs separated by ℓ\ellℓ bins. Only Nbin−ℓN_{\text{bin}} - \ellNbin​−ℓ bin pairs exist at that lag: the last ℓ\ellℓ bins have no partner. Dividing by this overlap converts the raw sum into an average product per available pair, removing the taper caused by finite recording length. Dividing again by ⟨nE⟩2{\langle n_{E} \rangle}^{2}⟨nE​⟩2 places independent firing near Acorr=1A_{\text{corr}} = 1Acorr​=1; this is a reference level, not a lower bound.

The Wiener–Khinchin route computes the lag products by zero-padding the count trace, taking its fast Fourier transform (FFT), multiplying by the complex conjugate and inverse-transforming. This costs O(nlog⁡n)O ( n \log n )O(nlogn) for all lags rather than a full O(n2)O ( n^{2} )O(n2) direct correlation, where OOO denotes asymptotic computational scaling.

A central lobe above chance describes short-lag spike clustering. A later trough can reflect suppression between volleys; a subsequent peak near the period is separate evidence of recurrence. Contrast is zero when its lobe and trough are equal, and reaches one if a positive lobe is paired with a zero trough. It summarizes their relative heights without measuring a stable oscillation frequency. Finite recordings, missing extrema and shared-input coincidence therefore matter when interpreting this scalar alongside rasters and longer-lag structure.
