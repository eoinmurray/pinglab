Abstract

We examined how inhibitory decay changes activity and decision-making in a trained spiking network. Longer decay slowed the network’s rhythm and reduced its spike rate. Slower networks took longer to classify images, but required roughly the same number of cycles. Longer decay also reduced spike cost, illustrating a trade-off between processing speed, accuracy and activity. These results concern one trained network.

Background

We imported the trained PING network from exp022 — MNIST and kept its weights fixed. The parameter table lists the network and simulation settings.

Inhibitory decay and network dynamics

We varied inhibitory decay in a separate overview sweep. The same frozen trained network was presented with ten paired encodings of one MNIST test image at a maximum pixel rate of 25 Hz. This broader 3–30 ms sweep provides context for the 3, 6 and 12 ms comparisons that follow.

Longer inhibitory decay reduced the spectral peak frequency and total excitatory spike rate in this sweep (Figure 1, A and C), while the proportion of excitatory neurons participating per cycle changed comparatively little (B). The rasters illustrate the wider spacing between volleys at longer decay times (E–G). These observations describe one image and one trained network.

Evidence across time

We encoded each of the first nine MNIST test images into 100 random spike trains and reused each train at τGABA=3,6,12\tau_{\mathrm{GABA}} = 3, 6, 12τGABA​=3,6,12 ms, where τGABA\tau_{\mathrm{GABA}}τGABA​ is the inhibitory decay time constant. Brighter pixels had higher average spike rates, with 25 spikes per second for a fully bright pixel. Each presentation lasted 200 ms, with 0.1 ms simulation steps. For each trial, the logit for digit ccc was its accumulated output spike count:

Here, ttt is elapsed time, Δtsim\Delta t_{\mathrm{sim}}Δtsim​ is the timestep, kkk indexes simulation steps, and sc[k]s_c[k]sc​[k] is one when output neuron ccc spikes, otherwise zero. We converted these logits into scores using softmax:

Here, pc(t)p_c(t)pc​(t) is the score for digit ccc, and jjj ranges over all ten digits. We averaged the correct digit’s score after softmax across 100 trials, separately for each decay setting. These scores are uncalibrated.

In Figure 2, we plot these mean correct-digit scores against elapsed time, with one panel per image and one curve per inhibitory decay setting. Each trace averages 100 random encoding seeds for the same image, with each seed reused across the three decay settings. Each point therefore summarises the relative evidence accumulated for the correct digit by that time. A higher curve means a larger mean softmax score for the correct digit; an earlier rise means that this evidence developed sooner. The curves compare the timing of evidence accumulation, rather than the fraction of trials classified correctly.

Evidence per completed volley

We detected inhibitory peaks after 1 ms Gaussian smoothing, requiring ≥2 ms spacing and prominence ≥max(1 spike, 20% of the maximum). Accepted sequences required a complete interior cycle and mean inhibitory participation ≥50%.

We defined each volley’s completion as the minimum of smoothed inhibitory activity between its peak and the next. At that boundary, we sampled the correct digit’s softmax score from all output spikes accumulated since onset.

We averaged these samples across 100 trials for each decay setting, using completed-volley counts available in every trial and setting. The final unbounded volley is excluded; count zero shows the initial score of 0.1 at time zero, not a detected volley. Clustering and score saturation can mimic alignment.

Sustained correct decisions

For the same nine images and 100 trials per decay setting, we found the earliest update at which the correct digit had the largest cumulative output spike count and remained the unique winner through 200 ms. Ties interrupt stability; trials without a stable correct decision were excluded.

We used the first completed-volley trough boundary at or after each stable decision. This is the first sampled point at which the decision is already stable, numbered from one. Counts required accepted volley sequences, a decision no later than the final completion boundary, and the shared completed-volley count range. We summarised median decision time and median completed-volley count against inhibitory decay. The two summaries can include different trial subsets. Trial counts are integers; medians can be fractional. Stability was assessed retrospectively, not by a stopping rule.

Accuracy at matched coordinates

We compared accuracy against time, cycle position and cumulative input spikes. The sample comprised 500 images, 50 per digit, with three paired encodings and inhibitory decay times of 3, 6 and 12 ms. Accuracy is omitted below 80% coverage. Later coordinates select subsets. Equal spike counts do not guarantee equal information.

Accuracy and spike cost

We compared mean network spikes versus elapsed time and accuracy versus spike cost at an encoding rate of 25 Hz and inhibitory decay times of 3, 6 and 12 ms. We evaluated five elapsed times within each setting, using 500 images and three paired encodings. Cost includes excitatory, inhibitory and output spikes, excluding input. Spike cost is not physical energy.
