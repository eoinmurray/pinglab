Abstract

We tested whether cue-selective synchronisation also selects sensory information. A coarse-to-fine search found settings with readable sender signals and an average cue benefit at the receiver. Receiver reconstruction remained weak and varied across fresh trials. Scrambling spike times improved overall reconstruction but removed the average cue preference. These results show a cue-dependent bias, rather than reliable selective information transfer or a clean demonstration of communication through coherence.

Results

With coupling

The fixed setting reproduced selective locking in both cue periods. Across the measured cue periods, selected-pair PLV was 0.960 for S1 and 0.961 for S2, versus 0.165 and 0.048 for the other sender. Selected-pair drift was below 0.03 cycles/s; the other sender drifted by 4–5 cycles/s. Brief high PLV before cueing did not persist across the baseline. In earlier checks without sensory modulations, none of three new-input trials passed every criterion: selected-pair PLV ranged from 0.819 to 0.978, with drift below 0.06 cycles/s. Selection persisted, but phase precision was not reliable under the fixed thresholds.

Without coupling

Without coupling, R stayed near 53 Hz while each cued sender reached about 55.5 Hz. Selected-pair PLV fell to 0.124 and 0.209, with drift of 2.96 and 2.42 cycles/s. Inputs and sender activity matched Figure 1 exactly; R was unaffected by the cues.

Cue bias without reliable transfer

The initial weak-signal test gave input R2R^2R2 scores of 0.597/0.410, sender scores of 0.017/−0.144 and cued receiver scores of −0.062/−0.117; cue benefits were −0.055/+0.095. After the search, fresh sender scores were 0.517/0.484, receiver cue benefits averaged 0.322/0.318, and cued reconstruction was 0.085/−0.008 (Figure 3B). Scrambling raised cued reconstruction to 0.203/0.148 but reversed mean cue benefits to −0.091/−0.165. Thus an average cue bias did not produce reliable reconstruction in both directions. These descriptive scores from three pairs, one wiring and one linear readout cannot exclude other codes.

Methods

  1. We used two sender networks, S1 and S2, and one receiver, R, with local settings inherited from exp133 — Lowet (2015): Coupled Rhythms. Each had 80 excitatory (E) cells and 20 inhibitory (I) cells. Separate groups of 32 cue cells excited their own sender.

  2. The synchrony reference ran continuously for 4 seconds per trial. The same cue input was sent first to S1, then to S2. All weights stayed fixed: sender-to-R connections were active from time zero in Figure 1 and zero throughout Figure 2. Background inputs were 33 Hz/channel for each sender and 35 Hz/channel for R. Coupling was 0.045 μS per pathway and cue strength was 0.06 μS. During each reference trial, only cue inputs changed; all other weights and inputs matched between the coupled and control trials.

  3. We measured timing from repeated bursts of excitatory spikes. Panel D in Figures 1–2 shows phase-locking value (PLV), using a centred 200 ms window every 10 ms:

    PLVSR(t)=∣1Nt∑k∈Wtexp⁡ ⁣(2πi[ϕS(k)−ϕR(k)])∣.\mathrm{PLV}_{SR}(t) = \left|\frac{1}{N_t}\sum_{k\in W_t} \exp\!\left(2\pi i[\phi_S(k)-\phi_R(k)]\right)\right|.PLVSR​(t)=​Nt​1​k∈Wt​∑​exp(2πi[ϕS​(k)−ϕR​(k)])​.

    Here, SSS is sender S1 or S2 and RRR is the receiver. The phases ϕS(k)\phi_S(k)ϕS​(k) and ϕR(k)\phi_R(k)ϕR​(k) are measured in cycles at time sample kkk. WtW_tWt​ contains the samples with valid phases in the 200 ms window centred at time ttt, and NtN_tNt​ is their number. The symbol iii is the imaginary unit; the outer bars take the magnitude, giving a value from 0 to 1. Only full windows with at least 90% valid timing were shown. Windows cross cue changes, smoothing transitions. Whole-period PLV and drift numbers exclude 100 ms after each change. Figures 1–2 show the input sequence used for selection.

  4. The fixed setting came from an earlier coarse-to-fine search following ter Wal and Tiesinga (2017). The reference pair reused the selected weights and original input sequence; the earlier new-input checks were not repeated.

  5. For the initial signal test, independent slow fluctuations changed each sender’s background input by at most 1.5 Hz/channel. Both signals were always present. Three training and three test input/noise sequences each appeared in both cue orders, with identical sensory spikes across orders. All neural weights stayed fixed. The original reference figures were reused, not rerun with these signals.

  6. Fixed ridge decoders reconstructed each signal from recent E/I population spike counts. Training alone set scaling and weights; one decoder per condition and signal was used across both cues. The same readout also checked inputs and senders. For each test sequence and cue, combining both orders, we measured

    R2=1−∑k∈T(sk−s^k)2∑k∈T(sk−sˉ)2.R^2 = 1 - \frac{\sum_{k\in\mathcal T}(s_k-\hat{s}_k)^2} {\sum_{k\in\mathcal T}(s_k-\bar{s})^2}.R2=1−∑k∈T​(sk​−sˉ)2∑k∈T​(sk​−s^k​)2​.

    Here T\mathcal TT is the set of test samples, kkk indexes those samples, sks_ksk​ is the signal value, s^k\hat{s}_ks^k​ its prediction, and sˉ\bar{s}sˉ its mean over that set. A score of one is exact reconstruction; zero matches the constant test mean. Cue benefit compares the same signal when cued and uncued. We required positive local and cued receiver scores, cue benefit in both directions, and improvement over zero coupling before calling the result selective routing.

  7. The signal trials included zero coupling and an artificial timing control. For the latter, native spike replay replaced emitted sender E spikes with independently shuffled times within 50 ms blocks. Every cell’s count in every block was preserved. Scrambling changes both rhythmic timing and coincident input, so it does not isolate phase alone.

  8. We started the new search at the Figure 1–2 setting. The coarse search added 16 combinations of signal strength, smoothing time, coupling and cue strength; the fine search added 16 combinations within ±35% of the chosen coarse values, plus the centre again. Mean drives and wiring stayed fixed. Each setting used one decoder-training and one validation signal/noise pair, both in both cue orders. Ranking first required both sender scores to reach R2≥0.1R^2\geq0.1R2≥0.1, then preferred positive cued receiver scores in both directions, then maximised the smaller same-signal cue benefit. Validation was reused during refinement.

  9. We tested the chosen setting with three fresh training and three fresh test pairs, again in both cue orders and all three coupling/timing conditions. Search and confirmation readouts used trailing 50 ms population rates at lags 0, 25 and 50 ms to predict the signal 50 ms earlier. Training alone set scaling and coefficients. We excluded the first 200 ms of each cue period and averaged scores across test pairs, rather than treating time samples as independent trials. No neural weights were trained. A timing-based interpretation also required readable scrambled sender signals and better cued reconstruction and cue benefit with intact timing.

The coarse points were a seeded Latin-hypercube sample, which spreads trials across each range. The fine sample used the same method within the smaller bounds. After readability and positive-score checks, selection maximised the smaller cue benefit. Remaining ties used the smaller cued receiver score, then the smaller sender score, then earlier candidate order. If no candidate met the readability threshold, the fallback would maximise the weaker sender score; it would not count as successful attention.

Connection masks and local weights were preserved. Coupling and cue weights changed only between settings; every weight remained constant during each trial. Figure 3 uses newly simulated signals and fresh tests; Figures 1–2 remain the earlier synchrony references. The scrambling control preserved exact per-neuron counts in every 50 ms block and retained mean sender reconstruction scores of 0.460/0.456. It reduced sender gamma power fractions from about 0.46 to 0.26, but also changed coincidences and sender-local dynamics, so it does not isolate inter-network phase alone. Test scores remain descriptive.
