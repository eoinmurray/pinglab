This investigation asks whether PING-generated gamma oscillations can emerge through task-driven optimisation and provide a computational advantage over an asynchronous irregular state. Networks would be trained under comparable resource constraints, rewarding task performance without explicitly rewarding oscillations. If PING repeatedly emerges and improves performance, and controlled disruption of its rhythmic timing removes that advantage, this would support PING as a discoverable, causally useful solution to the task. Such findings would lend plausibility to the hypothesis that gamma-generating circuitry can be favoured by natural selection for its computational benefits, without establishing that this explains its actual evolutionary origin.

The next goal is to identify a brain-relevant task for which PING might offer a specific, testable computational advantage. Task selection should begin with a computational demand that a particular brain circuit plausibly faces and a mechanistic prediction of how rhythmic excitation and inhibition could help meet it, rather than selecting a task solely because PING wins. Candidate demands include selective communication between competing inputs, detecting coincident inputs amid distractors, or transmitting information within a limited time and energy budget; these are hypotheses to assess, not established advantages of PING. The central question is under what biologically motivated task demands and constraints, if any, task-driven optimisation favours PING and whether its rhythmic timing causes the advantage. Finding no advantage, or finding that AI performs equally well, must remain a meaningful outcome.

The asynchronous irregular (AI) state is a sensible primary comparison, but it is not sufficient on its own. It represents an active, non-rhythmic regime, so it tests whether PING offers an advantage over a plausible alternative mode of network operation. The difficulty is that PING and AI can differ in firing rates, inhibition, correlations, and responsiveness as well as rhythmic timing; a performance difference would initially implicate the whole regime.

The strongest design would combine an AI comparison with a perturbation of the successful PING network that disrupts rhythmic coordination while preserving other properties as closely as possible. Letting training explore both regimes without prescribing either would also test whether task performance consistently favours PING. Thus, AI addresses “is PING better than this alternative?”, while the timing perturbation addresses “is its rhythmic organisation responsible?” The appropriate AI baseline should ultimately depend on the task and the biological circuit you intend to model.

## Perspectives

[Gould and Lewontin (1979), *The spandrels of San Marco and the Panglossian paradigm*](https://doi.org/10.1098/rspb.1979.0086), distinguish present utility from evolutionary origin. Their architectural analogy illustrates how a feature arising as a structural by-product can subsequently acquire a use. They criticise explanations that assume each trait is independently optimised, accept a plausible adaptive story without testing alternatives, or replace each failed story with another. This critique motivates a distinction between demonstrating a computational benefit and explaining the historical origin of biological gamma.

Applied to this investigation, three possibilities should remain explicit: rhythmic timing itself provides the task advantage; gamma accompanies another beneficial circuit property, such as inhibitory stabilisation or responsiveness; or gamma arose incidentally and was later recruited for computation. The first two concern the mechanism of present performance, whereas the third concerns historical origin and is compatible with present usefulness. A functional role and an incidental origin are therefore not mutually exclusive.

Repeated emergence during task-driven training would demonstrate discoverability under the model's architecture, constraints and optimisation procedure. It would not by itself show that rhythmic timing causes the benefit or that natural selection produced biological gamma for that benefit. Timing perturbations address the causal question within the model; comparative, developmental or other historical evidence would be needed to assess evolutionary explanations. Task selection should consequently follow an independently motivated biological demand, with credible alternatives and meaningful negative outcomes, rather than continue until a task is found on which PING wins.
