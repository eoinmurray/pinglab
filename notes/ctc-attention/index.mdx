## Abstract

## Background

Communication through coherence (CTC) proposes that rhythmic synchronisation
regulates communication between neuronal populations without changing their
anatomical connections. In Fries's account, gamma rhythms create brief excitatory
windows followed by longer inhibition; entrainment aligns incoming volleys with
these windows despite transmission delays, without requiring zero phase
difference. Representations may remain encoded in spatial firing-rate patterns,
while synchronisation determines their downstream influence. Attention is
proposed to bias this competition through gamma strength and frequency, with
alpha–beta feedback providing control and theta rhythms periodically reopening
selection \[[1](#ref-1)\].

### Mechanism

Let **A and B** represent competing stimuli and both send excitatory projections
to **C**. Each population contains excitatory and inhibitory neurons capable of
gamma dynamics. A and B have no direct connections to each other.

1. **Attention biases A upstream.** A control signal strengthens A's gamma
   synchronisation and/or makes its rhythm slightly faster. B remains active
   and continues sending input to C.
2. **A gains an arrival-time advantage.** Following a shared phase reset, A's
   faster rhythm can produce a synchronised volley that reaches C before B's.
   This advantage depends on the projection delays and circuit dynamics.
3. **A activates C, then recruits C's inhibition.** A's volley excites C's
   excitatory neurons, which recruit local inhibitory neurons. Fries describes
   excitation preceding inhibition by roughly 3 milliseconds. B's later volley
   encounters inhibition and therefore has less influence.
4. **C becomes entrained to A.** Subsequent A volleys arrive as C's inhibition
   decays, repeatedly finding an excitable window. C's output preferentially
   reflects A's representation, while B's input is suppressed.
5. **Selection can be reopened.** A theta-related reset may terminate the
   existing timing relationship and allow renewed competition. Selection can
   therefore persist across several gamma cycles without becoming permanent.\[[1](#ref-1)\]

## Proposed Experiment

We propose training the A/B → C network to classify one of two simultaneous
digits according to an attention cue. This adapts Bosman's competing-input task
to supervised classification; it is a proposed experiment, not a replication or
completed result \[[2](#ref-2)\].

1. **Sensory inputs.** Independently sample two 8×8 scikit-learn digits and encode
   each through 64 spike-input channels, one image into A and the other into B.
   Both images remain present during classification.
2. **Cue and target.** Cued control spikes favour A or B through the corresponding
   Attention input. The target is the cued image's digit class; changing the cue
   leaves both sensory inputs unchanged.
3. **Classification.** A ten-class readout receives activity only from C. Neither
   the images, A/B activity nor the cue bypass C to reach the readout. Train
   against the attended digit's label.
4. **Evaluation.** Split source images into training, validation and test sets
   before constructing pairs. Balance cue choices and digit classes. Test each
   held-out pair with both cues and identical sensory spike trains; for
   different-class pairs, the predicted class should switch with the cue.
5. **CTC tests.** After training, freeze the weights and apply the three tests
   below to held-out image pairs across training seeds.

### Testing CTC

Test whether gamma timing contributes to selective routing:

1. **Cue-dependent coupling.** With identical sensory inputs, changing the cue
   should switch both C's preferred gamma coupling and its classification.
   Compare with trial-shuffled controls, matching firing rates and gamma power.
2. **Phase-dependent transmission.** Identical weak probes should evoke different
   responses at different phases of C's gamma cycle. The cued sender's volleys
   should arrive preferentially at high-gain phases.
3. **Timing disruption and rescue.** Disrupt attended-input timing while preserving
   spike counts and within-volley synchrony, then restore it. Selection should
   deteriorate and recover beyond effects seen with sham, distractor and
   rate-matched nonoscillatory controls.

### Limitations

Classification or coherence alone cannot establish CTC.
Rate-based solutions, shared inputs and perturbation side effects require
controls. Positive results would support CTC in this model, not establish the
brain's attention mechanism.

### Plan

Proceed through six tasks, using mean-field models that retain E/I gamma
oscillations before testing the corresponding spiking circuits.

1. **Mean-field coupling.** Map stable gamma activity and entrainment across
   coupling strengths and measured uncoupled frequency differences, using A→C
   only with feedback disabled. Entrainment means C locks to A's frequency with
   a stable phase difference; their peaks need not coincide.

   firing rates, gamma power and perturbation effects.
