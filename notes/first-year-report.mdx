## 1. Background and literature review

### 1.1 Brief history of Gamma

<p>The term “gamma waves” dates to 1938, when Jasper and Andrews described occasional 35–45 Hz components in human electroencephalographic (EEG) recordings. They considered the activity insufficiently regular for systematic analysis. Its identification therefore preceded an account of either its circuit mechanism or its function [Ahmed and Cash (2013)](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnint.2013.00058).</p>

<p>Later experiments examined how gamma activity varied during sensory processing. [Di Prisco and Freeman (1985)](https://doi.org/10.1037/0735-7044.99.5.964) trained rabbits to discriminate an odour associated with water reward and recorded 40–80 Hz bursts through a 64-electrode array over the olfactory bulb. Differences between pre-odour and odour-period bursts were particularly evident during discrimination, associating gamma activity with learned sensory processing. In visual cortex, recordings from separated sites allowed researchers to examine coordination between populations. [Gray and colleagues (1989)](https://doi.org/10.1038/338334a0) presented moving bars to cats and compared simultaneously recorded spike trains. Synchronisation depended on stimulus configuration, with strong coordination when a continuous bar spanned separated receptive fields. [Figure 1](#gamma-synchrony) shows the near-zero-lag peak and oscillatory side peaks in the cross-correlation. These findings contributed to the temporal binding hypothesis: shared timing might group distributed responses into a common representation. Establishing such a role would require showing that perception depended on the coordination.</p>

<p>Attention provided a comparison in which the sensory display could remain unchanged. [Fries and colleagues (2001)](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1055465) recorded spikes and local field potentials in macaque visual area V4 while the animal attended different stimuli. Stronger gamma-frequency synchronisation for attended representations associated the rhythm with behavioural selection under matched sensory conditions.</p>

<p>Explaining these observations also required a circuit account of gamma generation. Pyramidal–interneuron network gamma (PING) describes a feedback cycle in which pyramidal firing recruits inhibitory interneurons, inhibition suppresses further excitation, and recovery permits another volley. [Börgers and Kopell (2003)](https://doi.org/10.1162/089976603321192059) examined this mechanism in simulations with sparse, random connectivity. Synchronisation persisted under suitable connectivity and synaptic conditions, extending the analysis beyond idealised all-to-all networks. Experimental evidence for the feedback account came from carbachol-induced gamma in rat hippocampal slices. Using electrode arrays, voltage-sensitive imaging and single-neuron recordings, [Mann and colleagues (2005)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2004.12.016) identified rhythmic inhibition around pyramidal cell bodies from perisomatic-targeting interneurons. Recurrent excitation mediated by AMPA receptors was necessary for synchronising interneuron discharge. The combination of excitatory recruitment and inhibitory feedback supported a PING-type mechanism in this preparation.</p>

<p>[Sohal and colleagues (2009)](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature07991) tested the feedback interaction directly by constructing an experimentally controlled loop in mouse cortical slices. They supplied non-rhythmic excitatory inputs to pyramidal neurons and used pyramidal spikes to trigger optogenetic activation of parvalbumin interneurons, which returned inhibition to the pyramidal cell. Adding the feedback enhanced gamma-frequency structure in pyramidal firing. This demonstrated how the excitatory–inhibitory interaction could generate rhythmic timing from non-rhythmic input, providing a causal test of the generative principle underlying PING.</p>

### 1.2 Gamma theories

<p>Gamma activity has been recorded in the neocortex, hippocampus, entorhinal cortex, olfactory bulb and amygdala, among other structures [Buzsáki and Wang (2012)](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-neuro-062111-150444). Its occurrence across these regions does not imply a single common generator. Local cellular architecture, synaptic organisation and patterns of input differ between circuits, so a shared frequency range can encompass distinct physiological processes.</p>

<p>In cortical and hippocampal circuits, pyramidal cells provide glutamatergic excitation, including fast excitation of interneurons through AMPA receptors. Interneurons release gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA), with fast inhibition through GABA<sub>A</sub> receptors contributing to gamma timing [Buzsáki and Wang (2012)](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-neuro-062111-150444). Fast-spiking, parvalbumin-expressing interneurons often target pyramidal cell bodies and nearby compartments, where inhibition can tightly regulate spike timing [Bartos and colleagues (2007)](https://doi.org/10.1038/nrn2044). Other cell types also contribute: dendrite-targeting somatostatin interneurons are important for a context-dependent visual gamma rhythm in mouse cortex [Veit and colleagues (2017)](https://doi.org/10.1038/nn.4562). The cells participating in gamma therefore depend on the circuit and stimulus conditions.</p>

<p>Gamma oscillations can arise through several interacting processes, but the principal network accounts are pyramidal–interneuron network gamma (PING) and interneuron network gamma (ING). They differ in which interactions sustain the rhythm.</p>

<p>In PING, pyramidal cells recruit inhibitory interneurons, whose feedback temporarily suppresses further pyramidal firing. As inhibition decays, sufficiently driven pyramidal cells can fire again and initiate another volley. The rhythm therefore emerges from repeated excitation, inhibition and recovery. Its frequency depends on inhibitory kinetics, synaptic delays, neuronal responsiveness and external drive. Individual pyramidal cells need not participate in every cycle: irregular cellular firing can coexist with rhythmic population activity. [Figure 2](#gamma-feedback) shows increased gamma-frequency power in pyramidal firing when feedback inhibition is added. [Börgers and Kopell (2003)](https://doi.org/10.1162/089976603321192059) demonstrated how this coordination can persist in sparsely and randomly connected networks.</p>

<p>Sustaining PING depends on the effective excitation from pyramidal cells to interneurons (E→I) and the inhibition returned to pyramidal cells (I→E). E→I weights govern interneuron recruitment, while I→E weights govern the strength of feedback inhibition. With suitable coupling and continuing drive, fixed weights can support the repeated cycle; synaptic modification is not required on each cycle. The two pathways have different effects: in sparse-network simulations, strengthening E→I connections improved synchronisation, whereas strengthening I→E connections did not provide the same benefit [Börgers and Kopell (2003)](https://doi.org/10.1162/089976603321192059).</p>

<p>In computational models, these weights may be chosen from physiological constraints, tuned to obtain a dynamical regime, or changed through training or explicit plasticity rules. These cases separate maintenance of a rhythm at fixed coupling from the question of how the circuit reaches that regime. Biological synapses can also change with experience: [Froemke and colleagues (2007)](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature06289) observed auditory cortical excitation being retuned, followed by activity-dependent changes in inhibition over hours. In modelling, inhibitory plasticity can establish and maintain a balance of excitatory and inhibitory inputs [Vogels and colleagues (2011)](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1211095). That study produced asynchronous irregular states, so regulating excitation–inhibition balance and preserving a PING regime are separate questions.</p>

<p>ING places the rhythm-generating interaction within the inhibitory population. Sufficiently driven interneurons coordinate through mutual inhibition, with recovery from one inhibitory volley permitting subsequent firing. Pyramidal cells may become phase-locked to the resulting rhythm without being required to generate it. [Wang and Buzsáki (1996)](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.16-20-06402.1996) showed in simulations that inhibitory networks could synchronise at gamma frequencies under suitable conditions of drive, connectivity and synaptic kinetics. Rhythmic pyramidal activity alone therefore does not identify a PING mechanism.</p>

<p>Cellular properties also shape these network dynamics. Membrane conductances can favour rhythmic bursting or responsiveness within particular frequency ranges, influencing neuronal recruitment and the timescales available to the circuit. Such intrinsic preferences require network interactions to explain coordinated population activity. [Buzsáki and Wang (2012)](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-neuro-062111-150444).</p>

<p>Electrical coupling provides another contribution. Gap junctions pass current directly between interneurons and can promote synchronisation alongside chemical inhibitory connections. They can therefore support inhibition-based gamma without constituting a separate generator. [Bartos and colleagues (2007)](https://doi.org/10.1038/nrn2044). These contributions can coexist, with the relative importance of PING, ING and cellular synchronising mechanisms changing across circuits and operating conditions. [Tiesinga and Sejnowski (2009)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2009.09.009).</p>

### 1.3 Proposed functional roles

<p>Gamma has been proposed to influence computation by organising when neurons fire and when their inputs are effective. These proposals range from local competition between cells to communication between areas, memory organisation and learning. They share an interest in temporal coordination, but make different predictions about what the coordination achieves.</p>

<p>At the local circuit level, rhythmic inhibition could select which cells participate in a response. In the E%-max mechanism proposed by [de Almeida and colleagues (2009)](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.6044-08.2009), strongly excited cells fire before inhibition prevents weaker competitors from responding. The resulting selection depends on relative excitation, allowing the participating population to change with the input. Related models connect gamma-mediated competition with stimulus selection [Börgers and Kopell (2008)](https://doi.org/10.1162/neco.2007.07-06-289).</p>

<p>Such selection could improve robustness by suppressing distracting activity or concentrate processing on strongly supported signals. Whether it also improves efficiency or latency requires a task-specific comparison with asynchronous activity. [Susin and Destexhe (2021)](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pcbi.1009416) show that integration, coincidence detection and resonance depend on network state. Different operations may therefore favour different regimes; fewer spikes alone would not establish an overall computational advantage.</p>

<p>Rhythmic inhibition also changes the conditions under which sensory input evokes a response. [Cardin and colleagues (2009)](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature08002) demonstrated that sensory-response amplitude and precision depended on input timing relative to an induced gamma cycle. Gamma could consequently provide recurring windows of responsiveness. This temporal gating is related to gamma-rhythmic gain modulation, in which response strength varies across the cycle [Ni and colleagues (2016)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2016.09.003).</p>

<p>Phase coding makes a further proposal: the timing of a spike within the cycle could itself represent information. Stimulus-related shifts in spike phase, such as those described by [Vinck and colleagues (2010)](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.1623-09.2010), motivate asking whether phase supplies information beyond firing rate. A phase-dependent response and a code read out through phase are distinct possibilities.</p>

<p>The same dependence on arrival time could regulate communication between populations. Communication through coherence proposes that inputs reaching a receiver during a more excitable phase exert greater influence [Fries (2015)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2015.09.034). Adjusting relative timing could therefore favour one of several converging signals without changing anatomical connections. This provides a proposed mechanism for attention and flexible routing. In [Figure 3](#gamma-attention), attention increases gamma-frequency spike–field coordination in V4. Routing effectiveness depends on how signals are organised: competing streams can interfere, and transmission delays alter the useful phase relationship [Akam and Kullmann (2012)](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pcbi.1002760). Increased coherence must consequently be distinguished from increased transmission of relevant information.</p>

<p>Temporal binding assigns synchrony a representational role. Neurons encoding different features of one object would share timing, distinguishing their activity from responses belonging to another object. The decoding requirements and experimental support for this proposal remain disputed [Shadlen and Movshon (1999)](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0896-6273(00)80822-3). Rate-based accounts instead express grouping through enhanced activity within assemblies [Roelfsema (2023)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2023.03.016).</p>

<p>Predictive-processing theories place gamma within exchanges between sensory evidence and expectations. One framework associates feedforward gamma with prediction-error signalling [Bastos and colleagues (2012)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2012.10.038). Other accounts connect gamma synchronisation with the integration of predictable visual structure [Vinck and Bosman (2016)](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnsys.2016.00035), while predictive routing concerns how expectations regulate communication [Bastos and colleagues (2020)](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2014868117). These accounts assign different roles to gamma, so they cannot be tested by treating gamma power as a general measure of prediction.</p>

<p>Memory theories use oscillatory timing to organise representations across timescales. Theta–gamma models propose that successive gamma cycles within a slower theta cycle can represent different items or positions in a sequence [Lisman and Jensen (2013)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2013.03.007). Different hippocampal gamma frequencies have also been associated with different communication pathways [Colgin and colleagues (2009)](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature08573). Working-memory recordings emphasising brief gamma and beta bursts broaden this picture beyond continuously maintained rhythms [Lundqvist and colleagues (2016)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2016.02.028).</p>

<p>Finally, coordinating spike timing could influence synaptic modification. Spike-timing-dependent plasticity establishes a dependence of synaptic change on relative presynaptic and postsynaptic timing [Bi and Poo (1998)](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.18-24-10464.1998), while experiments in oscillating visual cortical cells demonstrate phase-sensitive modification [Wespatat and colleagues (2004)](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.2221-04.2004). Gamma could thus help organise activity into relationships favourable for learning. Establishing when this occurs requires connecting the timing mechanism to changes in synaptic efficacy and subsequent task performance.</p>

### 1.4 Outstanding gaps

<p>The causal role of gamma remains difficult to isolate. Increased gamma-band power can reflect changes in aperiodic activity [Donoghue and colleagues (2020)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41593-020-00744-x), while coherence can arise from existing communication [Schneider and colleagues (2021)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2021.09.037). Interventions provide stronger evidence, but manipulating inhibitory neurons also changes firing rates and excitability. Timing-specific experiments narrow the question: [Drebitz and colleagues (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-025-62732-8) showed that behavioural disruption depended on receiver gamma phase. Establishing which computations require endogenous gamma coordination remains a task for experiments that distinguish timing effects from accompanying changes in activity.</p>

<p>The interpretation of these results also depends on how well models represent the relevant circuitry. PING and ING capture useful interactions, but simplify neuronal diversity, connectivity and spatial structure. Somatostatin-dependent visual gamma illustrates the contribution of inhibitory populations beyond those commonly represented in simplified models [Veit and colleagues (2017)](https://doi.org/10.1038/nn.4562). Transmission delays, changing frequencies and interference further constrain proposed routing mechanisms [Akam and Kullmann (2012)](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pcbi.1002760). Whether useful coordination can emerge through learning and remain effective under changing task demands is therefore an important extension of demonstrations using imposed rhythms.</p>

<p>Connecting a mechanism to a computational benefit requires explicit predictions about the information represented and how downstream circuits use it. Comparisons with asynchronous networks should provide comparable training opportunities, resources and inputs, with additional controls for firing rate and inhibition. Different operations may favour different regimes [Susin and Destexhe (2021)](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pcbi.1009416), so accuracy, robustness and latency need to be assessed alongside resource costs. Reduced spike counts alone do not establish energy savings. Benefits demonstrated in a particular task would identify useful operating conditions, while broader biological functions and evolutionary explanations would require further evidence.</p>

## 2. Methods

### 2.1 Simulation

<p>Simulation makes a proposed mechanism explicit by specifying its cells, connections and dynamics, then examining the behaviour produced by their interaction. It links cellular assumptions to population activity and computational outcomes, while allowing controlled changes to individual circuit properties. This supports an iterative relationship with experiment: observations constrain candidate models, and simulations generate predictions that distinguish their explanations [Einevoll and colleagues (2019)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2019.03.027).</p>

<p>Spiking neural networks are useful when the question depends on individual spike timing and its interaction with membrane and synaptic dynamics. They allow inhibitory feedback, transmission delays, irregular firing and synchronisation to be represented explicitly. Integrate-and-fire neurons retain these temporal interactions while simplifying the cellular processes that produce each spike [Gerstner and colleagues (2014)](https://neuronaldynamics.epfl.ch/online/Ch1.S3.html). For PING, this makes it possible to examine how excitatory recruitment and inhibitory recovery generate a population rhythm, and how the resulting timing influences downstream responses. Population-rate models can also produce oscillations; the choice of a spiking model therefore rests on the temporal and cellular questions being investigated.</p>

<p>The resulting evidence is conditional on the model assumptions. Reproducing gamma activity establishes that a mechanism can generate the observation; identifying the biological mechanism requires further comparison with experimental data. Perturbing coordination within a model can test its computational consequences, with controls to distinguish timing effects from changes in rates, inhibition and excitability. Numerical implementation also requires attention because integration strategy and temporal resolution can affect spike timing and network dynamics [Brette and colleagues (2007)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2638500/).</p>

### 2.2 Circuits

<p>The circuit description follows the conductance-based spiking model documented in the [manuscript’s Methods](/notes/manuscript#loc-20). Excitatory pyramidal cells (E) receive external spike input and recruit an inhibitory population (I), which returns inhibition to E. The classifier architecture contains input→E, E→I, I→E and E→readout projections, with E→E and I→I recurrence disabled. The loop-on condition retains the reciprocal E–I connections; loop-off removes both pathways. These are simplified population models, with each hidden neuron represented by a single membrane voltage.</p>

<p>Between spikes, a hidden neuron follows conductance-based leaky integrate-and-fire dynamics. Synaptic input changes both the current entering the cell and its total membrane conductance:</p>

<span id="circuit-membrane" />
<EquationBox label="Conductance-based membrane dynamics">

$$
C_m \frac{\mathrm{d}V}{\mathrm{d}t}
= -g_L(V-E_L)-g_E(V-E_E)-g_I(V-E_I).
\tag{1}
$$

</EquationBox>

<p>Here, $V$ is membrane voltage and $t$ is time; $C_m$ is membrane capacitance; $g_L$ is leak conductance; and $g_E$ and $g_I$ are the time-varying excitatory and inhibitory synaptic conductances. The corresponding reversal potentials are $E_L$, $E_E$ and $E_I$. Voltages are expressed in mV, time in ms, capacitance in nF and conductances in µS, giving each term in [Equation 1](#circuit-membrane) units of nA. The products $g_E(V-E_E)$ and $g_I(V-E_I)$ retain the dependence of synaptic current on the neuron’s voltage, including the effect of inhibition on membrane conductance.</p>

<p>Outside its refractory period, a neuron emits a spike when its voltage reaches the threshold $V_{\mathrm{th}}$. Its voltage is then set to $V_{\mathrm{reset}}$ and held there for an absolute refractory duration $\tau_{\mathrm{ref}}$, measured in ms. Both voltage parameters are in mV. Synaptic conductances continue evolving during this hold. This threshold-and-reset rule represents spike timing without modelling the action-potential waveform [Gerstner and colleagues (2014)](https://neuronaldynamics.epfl.ch/online/Ch1.S3.html).</p>

<p>Each incoming spike produces a conductance increment, while existing conductance decays exponentially. For a particular postsynaptic neuron, the discrete update is</p>

<span id="circuit-synapses" />
<EquationBox label="Synaptic conductance update">

$$
g_x[k+1]
= g_x[k]\exp\!\left(-\frac{\Delta t_{\mathrm{sim}}}{\tau_x}\right)
+ \sum_j w_{xj}s_j[k],
\qquad x\in\{E,I\}.
\tag{2}
$$

</EquationBox>

<p>The index $k$ counts simulation updates, and $\Delta t_{\mathrm{sim}}$ is the timestep in ms. The label $x$ identifies the excitatory or inhibitory conductance, rather than the postsynaptic population. The decay constant $\tau_x$ is in ms; $w_{xj}$ is the non-negative conductance increment in µS from presynaptic neuron $j$; and $s_j[k]\in\{0,1\}$ indicates a spike delivered in that update. The sum runs over sources of the corresponding synaptic type. Excitatory and inhibitory decay constants represent AMPA- and GABA-mediated synaptic timescales. In the manuscript implementation, decay precedes spike increments, updated conductances are used for exponential-Euler voltage integration, and newly emitted recurrent spikes reach the partner population on the following update. This ordering is specified in the [manuscript’s discrete dynamics](/notes/manuscript#appendix-a1).</p>

<p>The reciprocal connections use separate weight distributions, allowing E→I recruitment and I→E feedback to be varied independently. For these recurrent projections, the manuscript’s initialisation is</p>

<span id="circuit-weights" />
<EquationBox label="Recurrent synaptic weight initialisation">

$$
X_{ji}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{\mathrm{init}},\sigma_{\mathrm{init}}^2),
\qquad
w_{ji}=\frac{\max(0,X_{ji})}{N_{\mathrm{pre}}}.
\tag{3}
$$

</EquationBox>

<p>Here, $j$ indexes the presynaptic neuron and $i$ the postsynaptic neuron. The Gaussian draw $X_{ji}$, parent mean $\mu_{\mathrm{init}}$, parent standard deviation $\sigma_{\mathrm{init}}$ and resulting weight $w_{ji}$ are in µS; $N_{\mathrm{pre}}$ is the number of neurons in the source population. Lower clamping keeps conductance increments non-negative, while division by $N_{\mathrm{pre}}$ normalises their scale with population size. Excitatory and inhibitory actions are specified by the synaptic reversal potentials, rather than by assigning negative inhibitory weights. The more general input-weight initialisation additionally includes random zeroing and compensation for that zeroing, as specified in the [manuscript’s initialisation procedure](/notes/manuscript#appendix-a4).</p>

<p>Together, the reciprocal connections and synaptic timescales permit the excitation–inhibition–recovery sequence associated with PING. They do not guarantee that every parameter setting produces gamma: coupling, external drive and cellular properties determine the resulting regime. Loop-off is therefore an architectural control; asynchronous irregular activity must be established from the simulated activity rather than assumed from the absence of the loop.</p>

### 2.3 Training

<p>The training description follows the manuscript’s [MNIST classifier protocol](/notes/manuscript#loc-21). Most comparisons trained the input→E and E→readout weights while holding E→I and I→E coupling fixed. A separate comparison also trained the reciprocal connections to examine how their modification affected the circuit. Independent image presentations began with freshly initialised hidden and output states. Continuous-stream evaluation instead retained hidden state between images.</p>

<p>Pixel intensities were divided by 255 and converted into independent stochastic spike channels:</p>

<span id="training-encoding" />
<EquationBox label="Stochastic input encoding">

$$
s_j[k]\sim\operatorname{Bernoulli}(p_j),
\qquad
p_j=a_j r_{\mathrm{input,max}}\Delta t_{\mathrm{sim}}.
\tag{4}
$$

</EquationBox>

<p>Here, $j$ indexes an input pixel, $k$ the simulation update, $s_j[k]\in\{0,1\}$ its spike indicator, and $a_j\in[0,1]$ its normalised intensity. The maximum-pixel firing rate $r_{\mathrm{input,max}}$ is in Hz. In [Equation 4](#training-encoding), $\Delta t_{\mathrm{sim}}$ is expressed in seconds, making $p_j$ a dimensionless per-step probability. The product must lie between zero and one. Fresh draws were generated across pixels, timesteps and training presentations.</p>

<p>Ten dimensionless, current-based spiking output units received weighted excitatory spikes, one unit per digit class. Their states decayed exponentially, integrated incoming spikes and underwent subtractive reset when they emitted an output spike. Ordinary classification used the mean state before reset as the class score. With minibatch cross-entropy,</p>

<span id="training-objective" />
<EquationBox label="Readout scores and classification objective">

$$
\begin{aligned}
z_{b,c}
&=\frac{1}{N_t}\sum_{k=1}^{N_t}\tilde{u}_{b,c}[k],\\
L_{\mathrm{CE}}
&=-\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}
\log\!\left(\frac{\exp(z_{b,y_b})}{\sum_{c=0}^{9}\exp(z_{b,c})}\right).
\end{aligned}
\tag{5}
$$

</EquationBox>

<p>The index $b$ identifies a presentation in a minibatch of size $B$, $c\in\{0,\ldots,9\}$ identifies a class, and $N_t$ is the number of simulation steps per presentation. The dimensionless state $\tilde{u}_{b,c}[k]$ is the output state before threshold subtraction, $z_{b,c}$ is its mean score, and $y_b$ is the true digit label. The predicted class maximises $z_{b,c}$; $L_{\mathrm{CE}}$ is the dimensionless mean cross-entropy. The manuscript’s continuous-stream classifiers instead used accumulated output-spike counts as scores. The exact output recurrence is given in the [manuscript’s readout specification](/notes/manuscript#appendix-a2).</p>

<p>Optimisation used full, untruncated backpropagation through the temporal unrolling of each presentation. Forward spikes remained binary. During the backward pass, the non-differentiable threshold operation was assigned a fast-sigmoid surrogate derivative, following the surrogate-gradient approach reviewed by [Neftci and colleagues (2019)](https://doi.org/10.1109/MSP.2019.2931595):</p>

<span id="training-surrogate" />
<EquationBox label="Surrogate spike derivative">

$$
\frac{\partial s}{\partial\xi}\approx\psi(\xi)
=\frac{\beta}{(1+\beta|\xi|)^2}.
\tag{6}
$$

</EquationBox>

<p>Here, $s$ is the binary spike indicator, $\xi$ is the numerical distance from threshold, and $\psi$ is the surrogate derivative used in backpropagation. Hidden-neuron distances were evaluated numerically in mV and output-neuron distances in normalised state units; the surrogate slope parameter was $\beta=1$ in those numerical conventions. This supplies a backward sensitivity around threshold without replacing the forward spike with a smooth-valued output.</p>

<p>The manuscript additionally damped gradients through each hidden E and I membrane increment. This was an implementation-specific modification of the backward calculation, expressed using a stop-gradient operator:</p>

<span id="training-damping" />
<EquationBox label="Hidden-voltage gradient damping">

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{D}_{\alpha_{\mathrm{grad}}}(x)
&=\alpha_{\mathrm{grad}}x
 +(1-\alpha_{\mathrm{grad}})\operatorname{sg}(x),\\
\alpha_{\mathrm{grad}}&=\frac{1}{d_{\mathrm{grad}}},
\qquad d_{\mathrm{grad}}=1000,\\
\bar{V}[k+1]&=V[k]+\mathcal{D}_{\alpha_{\mathrm{grad}}}(F[k]).
\end{aligned}
\tag{7}
$$

</EquationBox>

<p>The operation $\operatorname{sg}(x)$ returns $x$ in the forward pass and has zero backward derivative. The dimensionless divisor $d_{\mathrm{grad}}$ therefore gives $\alpha_{\mathrm{grad}}=10^{-3}$. Here, $x$ denotes the argument of the damping operation; in the voltage update it is $F[k]$, the unclipped exponential-Euler voltage increment computed from [Equation 1](#circuit-membrane), with updated conductances held constant over the timestep. The quantity $\bar{V}[k+1]$ is voltage before clipping, reset and refractory handling. Thus, $x$, $F[k]$, $V[k]$ and $\bar{V}[k+1]$ are in mV. Forward values are preserved, while derivatives through $F[k]$, including its dependence on conductances and previous voltage, are multiplied by $\alpha_{\mathrm{grad}}$. The additive $V[k]$ path retains unit derivative.</p>

<p>At fixed updated conductances, this produces the backward voltage sensitivity</p>

<span id="training-damped-jacobian" />
<EquationBox label="Backward sensitivity of hidden voltage">

$$
\left(\frac{\partial\bar{V}[k+1]}{\partial V[k]}\right)_{\mathrm{BP}}
=1-\alpha_{\mathrm{grad}}\left[
1-\exp\!\left(-\frac{\Delta t_{\mathrm{sim}}g_{\mathrm{tot}}[k+1]}{C_m}\right)
\right].
\tag{8}
$$

</EquationBox>

<p>The subscript $\mathrm{BP}$ identifies the derivative used by backpropagation. Total conductance is $g_{\mathrm{tot}}[k+1]=g_L+g_E[k+1]+g_I[k+1]$, in µS. Here, $\Delta t_{\mathrm{sim}}$ is again in ms and $C_m$ is in nF, as in section 2.2, so the exponential argument is dimensionless. The same damping applied to both hidden populations in loop-on and loop-off networks. Conductance recurrences and the surrogate spike function received no separate damping factor, and output-state updates were undamped. Hidden reset and refractory masks were not differentiated; the eligible spike-output branch retained surrogate gradients, while reset, refractory holds and voltage clipping blocked the corresponding retained-voltage gradients. These paths are specified in the [manuscript’s gradient calculations](/notes/manuscript#appendix-a3).</p>

<p>For comparisons of accuracy and excitatory activity, the manuscript supplemented cross-entropy with a one-sided rate penalty:</p>

<span id="training-rate-penalty" />
<EquationBox label="Optional excitatory-rate penalty">

$$
L_{\mathrm{total}}=L_{\mathrm{CE}}
+\frac{\lambda_{\mathrm{rate}}}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}
\left[\frac{\max(0,r_{E,b}-r_{E,\mathrm{ceil}})}{1\,\mathrm{Hz}}\right]^2.
\tag{9}
$$

</EquationBox>

<p>The rate $r_{E,b}$ is the excitatory spike count for presentation $b$, divided by excitatory population size and duration in seconds; $r_{E,\mathrm{ceil}}$ is the selected reference ceiling, also in Hz. Division by $1\,\mathrm{Hz}$ makes the excess dimensionless, and $\lambda_{\mathrm{rate}}$ sets its dimensionless penalty strength. Setting $\lambda_{\mathrm{rate}}=0$ recovers unpenalised training. The penalty is applied to each presentation before minibatch averaging and does not enforce a hard firing-rate limit.</p>

<p>The documented protocol used 50 epochs of AdamW with learning rate 0.0004, minibatches of 256 images and zero weight decay. Before each update, the global Euclidean gradient norm was clipped to a maximum of one; trainable weights were subsequently clamped below at zero. This global clipping was separate from the local membrane-gradient damping above. Checkpoint selection minimised held-out validation cross-entropy, averaged over three spike encodings per image with seeds fixed across epochs. The activity penalty was excluded from validation loss. The test partition remained separate from optimisation and checkpoint selection. Analyses using final-epoch networks were distinguished from those using the best-validation checkpoint.</p>

### 2.4 Toolbox and implementation

<p>[SNNLab](https://github.com/eoinmurray/snnlab) provides the software framework for constructing, simulating and training the spiking circuits described above. It is implemented in Python and PyTorch, with neuronal states and synaptic interactions represented as tensor operations that support GPU execution and automatic differentiation. Network definitions specify populations, connections, synaptic dynamics and transmission delays, while execution settings control input presentation, recording and training. This separation allows circuit architecture and experimental conditions to be varied within a common numerical implementation.</p>

<p>The library also supports recording spikes, membrane voltages and synaptic conductances, alongside analysis of population firing rates and rhythmic activity. These measurements connect task performance to the underlying circuit dynamics: a change in classification accuracy can be examined alongside changes in inhibitory feedback, excitability and population timing.</p>

<p>Verification addresses both the simulated dynamics and the procedures used to train and restore networks. The [simulator test suite](https://github.com/eoinmurray/snnlab/tree/main/tests/sim) covers neuronal and synaptic updates, spike and refractory timing, surrogate gradients, checkpoint restoration and continued training. Numerical checks include analytical solutions and preserved reference outputs, together with independent [Brian2 comparisons for conductance-based dynamics](https://github.com/eoinmurray/snnlab/blob/main/tests/sim/test_brian2_conformance.py) and [snnTorch comparisons for current-based dynamics](https://github.com/eoinmurray/snnlab/blob/main/tests/sim/test_snntorch_conformance.py). Such comparisons require matched equations, parameters and update conventions, since differences in spike timing can arise from the ordering of otherwise similar numerical operations.</p>

<p>Saved network definitions, checkpoints and recordings make the model and its outputs available for inspection. Reproducing an experiment additionally requires its software revision, numerical settings, random seeds and training configuration, together with the checkpoint-selection and evaluation procedures. These details identify the particular implementation and protocol underlying each reported result.</p>

## 3. Research Plan

### 3.1 Efficiency and robustness

<p>The first strand of work asks whether PING can support useful computation with fewer excitatory spikes, and how that computation depends on the strength and duration of its input. The completed experiments establish a low-rate rhythmic operating regime, test its accuracy–activity trade-off in digit classification, and examine performance when inputs arrive as continuous streams. The figures below reproduce the corresponding results from the [manuscript](/notes/manuscript).</p>

<p>The starting comparison used untrained conductance-based networks with reciprocal E→I and I→E coupling either enabled or disabled. With the loop disabled, excitatory spikes were dispersed and inhibitory neurons remained silent. Enabling the loop produced recurring excitatory and inhibitory volleys, with an excitatory-population spectral peak near {display(round(circuitEvidence.f_gamma_hz.ping, 1))} Hz ([Figure 4](#efficiency-circuit)C–E,G). The two illustrative rasters used different input rates, chosen to display the contrasting regimes. Their firing rates therefore cannot be treated as a matched-input comparison.</p>

<p>The input–output sweeps provide that comparison. Across the same input-rate range, loop-off excitatory firing rose from {display(round(Math.min(...circuitEvidence.fi_curves.coba.e), 1))} to {display(round(Math.max(...circuitEvidence.fi_curves.coba.e), 1))} Hz, whereas loop-on firing remained between {display(round(Math.min(...circuitEvidence.fi_curves.ping.e), 1))} and {display(round(Math.max(...circuitEvidence.fi_curves.ping.e), 1))} Hz ([Figure 4](#efficiency-circuit)F,H). Inhibitory firing increased with drive, showing that the low excitatory rate was maintained alongside greater recruitment of the inhibitory population.</p>

<p>Continuous-stream classification extends the result beyond independently reset inputs, but these experiments do not show that retaining hidden state improves accuracy or that PING is more robust than another circuit: neither comparison was made. Presentation duration and readout accumulation time also changed together. The uncertainty across three trained networks is conditional on one sampled image bank, and supplied boundaries leave autonomous segmentation unresolved.</p>

<p>The next stage should separate these contributions. Comparisons that match excitatory and inhibitory activity, alongside controlled changes to inhibitory timing, would test whether temporal organisation contributes beyond activity suppression. Streaming controls that reset hidden state, vary preceding-image order and separate stimulus duration from the readout window would examine carry-over and evidence accumulation. Testing additional image banks and input perturbations would then assess how broadly the observed operating regime supports reliable computation. This would turn the present demonstration of low-rate task performance into a more direct test of efficiency and robustness.</p>

### 3.2 Selective communication and attention

<p>The second strand asks whether a receiver can preferentially represent one of two simultaneous inputs, and whether rhythmic timing helps organise that selection. Communication through coherence proposes that a sender’s influence depends partly on its alignment with fluctuations in a receiver’s excitability [Fries (2005)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2005.08.011). [Experiment 172](/exp172) examines this question in a fixed spiking circuit, using distinguishable spatial patterns to measure what the receiver represents.</p>

<p>Two sender areas, A and B, project to a shared receiver, C ([Figure 8](#attention-circuit)). Each area contains excitatory and inhibitory neurons with local recurrence. A and B receive simultaneous Gaussian profiles of sensory firing rates, centred at different positions on a circular spatial map. Fixed sensory and sender-to-receiver weights favour nearby spatial positions; the A→C and B→C matrices are identical. A spatially uniform cue adds {attentionExample.cue_hz} Hz per channel to the attended sender, switching from A to B at {attentionConfig.switch_ms} ms while both sensory patterns remain present. The cue changes sender drive without specifying which spatial neurons should fire.</p>

<PublishedFigure experiment="exp172" file="circuit.svg" id="attention-circuit" alt="Saved native A/B-to-C spatial relay circuit with separate sensory and uniform cue inputs and local excitatory–inhibitory recurrence.">
  **Figure 8. Circuit for selecting between simultaneous spatial inputs.** From [exp172 Figure 1](/exp172#fig-circuit). Each area contains {attentionConfig.network.populations.E.size} E and {attentionConfig.network.populations.I.size} I neurons; each external stream has {attentionConfig.network.input_channels} channels. X denotes sensory input to A/B and uniform background input to C; U denotes the cue. Complementary gates route the cue to A before the switch and B afterwards. E populations are black and I populations red. Weight subscripts identify target then source; grey sender-E→C-I paths have zero weights. All weights are fixed, with no task learning.
</PublishedFigure>

<p>Selection is decoded from complete C excitatory-population cycles, bounded by troughs between successive detected peaks. Within each cycle, the spatial firing-rate profile is fitted by nonnegative least squares to a uniform background and fixed A/B Gaussian templates. The larger fitted pattern amplitude determines dominance. A cycle is unresolved if the amplitudes tie or their sum is below {attentionConfig.minimum_total_amplitude_hz} Hz. The following three operational metrics distinguish correctness, stability and response onset; they are the definitions used in this experiment.</p>

<p>For cue period $e$, selection success is the fraction of retained cycles dominated by the attended pattern:</p>

<span id="attention-success" />
<EquationBox label="Selection success">

$$
S_e=\frac{N_{\mathrm{correct},e}}{N_e}.
\tag{10}
$$

</EquationBox>

<p>Here, $e$ identifies the attend-A or attend-B period, $N_e$ counts complete C cycles within its settled measurement window, and $N_{\mathrm{correct},e}$ counts those dominated by the attended pattern. Counts and $S_e$ are dimensionless. Unresolved cycles remain in the denominator and count as failures; no retained cycles makes the score unavailable. A score of one means every retained cycle is correct. When the experiment summarises both periods with one success score, it averages their two scores equally.</p>

<p>Flickering measures changes in the dominant pattern between adjacent resolved cycles within a cue period:</p>

<span id="attention-flicker" />
<EquationBox label="Cycle-to-cycle flickering">

$$
F_e=\frac{N_{\mathrm{changes},e}}{N_{\mathrm{pairs},e}}.
\tag{11}
$$

</EquationBox>

<p>The count $N_{\mathrm{pairs},e}$ includes adjacent cycle pairs for which both patterns are resolved, and $N_{\mathrm{changes},e}$ counts pairs whose dominance differs. Unresolved cycles break adjacency, and pairs do not cross cue-period boundaries. The dimensionless score ranges from zero for unchanged dominance to one for alternation at every eligible boundary; it is unavailable without eligible pairs. A stable wrong response can therefore have low flickering and low success. If periods are pooled, their change counts and eligible-pair counts are summed before division.</p>

<p>Latency records the beginning of the first run of three consecutive attended-dominant cycles after cue onset:</p>

<span id="attention-latency" />
<EquationBox label="Sustained-selection onset latency">

$$
L_e=\min\!\left\{
k\in\{1,\ldots,M_e-2\}:
z_{e,k}=z_{e,k+1}=z_{e,k+2}=a_e
\right\}.
\tag{12}
$$

</EquationBox>

<p>Here, $M_e$ is the number of complete C cycles wholly contained in the full cue period, $k$ indexes them from one, $z_{e,k}$ is cycle $k$’s dominant-pattern label, and $a_e\in\{A,B\}$ is the attended label. Unlike the settled windows used for success and flickering, latency includes the initial response after cue onset. A cycle straddling onset is excluded, and an unresolved cycle breaks the run. $L_e$ is reported in cycles: one means the first eligible cycle begins the qualifying run, rather than zero elapsed time. If no run occurs before the period ends, latency is not observed. A short latency does not guarantee that selection remains stable afterwards.</p>

<p>The uniform-cue example shows C’s spatial response shifting with the cue, with intermittent excursions towards the competing pattern ([Figure 9](#attention-uniform)). For this displayed seed-{attentionExample.seed} trial, success was {display(round(uniformSelection('A').S, 2))} during attend A and {display(round(uniformSelection('B').S, 2))} during attend B; flickering was {display(round(uniformSelection('A').F, 2))} and {display(round(uniformSelection('B').F, 2))}, respectively. The corresponding onset latencies were {uniformSelection('A').latency_cycles} and {uniformSelection('B').latency_cycles} cycles, illustrating why response onset and subsequent stability require separate measures.</p>

<p>The timing analysis relates this changing representation to sender–receiver phase ([Figure 10](#attention-phase)). Dominant patterns cluster around a preferred wrapped phase, while the unwrapped traces retain whole-cycle drift. The plotted local phase locking, $E(t)$, is a trailing phase-locking value over {attentionExample.entrainment.window_cycles} receiver-peak samples, with $t$ denoting time in ms. It is the length of the mean unit vector formed from the sampled phase differences, a moving-window application of the [phase-locking value](https://doi.org/10.1002/%28SICI%291097-0193%281999%298:4%3C194::AID-HBM4%3E3.0.CO;2-C). Values near one indicate a stable circular phase relationship; they do not identify which spatial pattern dominates. Integer-cycle slips are also invisible to this circular statistic.</p>

<p>The grey phase band was measured through an existing intervention, rather than inferred from inhibitory spike times. Each naturally occurring sender volley was removed separately from its input to C, with an exact matched continuation from the same native state. Spatial-pattern losses were measured over one and two complete C cycles using fixed baseline boundaries. The retained effect was the minimum of the removed sender’s own-pattern loss and its own-minus-competing-pattern loss across both horizons, with the two-cycle result normalised per cycle. This tests whether a packet supports its spatial representation beyond a brief timing displacement. The [full procedure](/exp172#used-transmission-measurement) identifies strong transmission phases used in this recording; it does not locate a universal receptive-window boundary.</p>

<p>A complementary condition replaced the uniform cue with a change in sensory contrast. The attended Gaussian’s modulation amplitude increased while its uniform baseline decreased, preserving each sensory input’s expected spatial mean at {attentionContrast.configuration.spatial_mean_hz} Hz/channel ([Figure 11](#attention-contrast-profiles)). The contrast ratio is the attended-to-unattended modulation-amplitude ratio, not a ratio of spatial mean rates. Gaussian widths, weights and the unattended profile remained fixed. This asks whether redistributing a fixed mean sensory drive towards the represented location can bias selection.</p>

<p>The uniform and contrast conditions therefore demonstrate two ways of biasing a fixed circuit’s spatial response. They use different receiver backgrounds—{attentionExample.background_hz} and {attentionContrast.settings.background_hz} Hz/channel—so they are not a matched comparison of cue mechanisms. Matching external sensory means also leaves sender firing rates and temporal structure free to change. Spatial decoding measures which pattern C expresses; the packet-deletion analysis supplies separate evidence about the contribution of particular sender volleys. Neither alone establishes that phase alignment is sufficient for selective communication.</p>

<p>Further work should test this relationship across held-out input realisations and independently initialised circuits. Paired timing interventions that preserve sender spike counts, projection weights and receiver starting state would help distinguish temporal alignment from increased effective drive. Testing whether the measured transmission-phase dependence predicts the consequences of these interventions would provide a stronger link between PING dynamics and selective communication. The present results establish a controllable but imperfect spatial relay, with explicit measures of its correctness, instability and response onset.</p>

### 3.3 Perceptual binding and grouping

<p>A proposed extension would test whether temporal coordination helps a downstream circuit group features belonging to the same object. The task would present two objects simultaneously, with different conjunctions of features such as colour and orientation. Individual feature identities and mean input rates would be held constant while their grouping changed. A readout would have to recover the correct conjunctions, making grouping errors distinguishable from failures to detect the individual features. This addresses the decoding requirement central to criticisms of temporal binding [Shadlen and Movshon (1999)](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0896-6273(00)80822-3).</p>

<p>The first simulation would impose controlled timing relationships between feature populations and test whether a downstream circuit could read them. Relative shifts of complete spike trains would change their alignment while preserving individual spike counts and within-population temporal structure. Performance would be compared with a rate-based readout and tested on held-out feature combinations. This would identify the timing precision, connectivity and readout operations required for a synchrony-based grouping code.</p>

<p>A subsequent model would ask whether coupled PING circuits could generate the useful coordination from sensory structure without supplying object membership through imposed synchrony. Separating generation from decoding would clarify which parts of a binding mechanism the circuit actually explains.</p>

### 3.4 Sensory gain, temporal gating and phase coding

<p>A further simulation would map how a PING receiver responds to inputs of different strengths arriving at different phases of its ongoing activity. Identical probe volleys would be delivered across the cycle, accounting for transmission delays when measuring arrival phase. Spike probability, response magnitude and first-spike latency would be recorded alongside membrane voltage and excitatory and inhibitory conductances. Input–output curves at each phase would distinguish changes in response slope from shifts in firing threshold. This would provide a circuit-level counterpart to the phase-dependent sensory responses reported by [Cardin and colleagues (2009)](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature08002) and gamma-rhythmic gain modulation examined by [Ni and colleagues (2016)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2016.09.003).</p>

<p>The same framework could then test whether response phase carries stimulus information beyond spike count. Held-out decoding would compare counts alone with counts plus phase, using a phase reference estimated from activity preceding the probe. Rhythmic and aperiodic controls would be matched as closely as possible for mean inhibition and baseline firing. These comparisons could distinguish a recurring window of responsiveness from a phase code that adds information useful to a downstream circuit.</p>

### 3.5 Prediction-error signalling and alternative predictive functions

<p>The proposed predictive-processing study would place a sensory PING circuit within a small hierarchy containing a learned predictor and feedback representing the expected input. Predictive coding associates feedforward gamma with prediction-error signalling [Bastos and colleagues (2012)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2012.10.038), whereas predictable-structure accounts emphasise the integration of regular sensory features [Vinck and Bosman (2016)](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnsys.2016.00035). A third possibility is that expectations regulate routing between populations [Bastos and colleagues (2020)](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2014868117). The simulations would seek conditions under which these accounts make distinguishable predictions.</p>

<p>After learning regular sequences, the model would encounter expected inputs, substitutions and omissions. The same physical stimulus would be presented under different expectations, with controls for recent repetition history and sensory adaptation. Analyses would test whether gamma-related activity carries the observed stimulus, the mismatch from its prediction, or the signal selected for onward transmission. Message identity would be assessed through decoding and pathway interventions, rather than inferred from gamma power alone.</p>

<p>This could establish which circuit operations are needed for each proposed function and identify observations that separate them. In particular, an omission response could help distinguish internally generated mismatch activity from a response requiring an incoming sensory volley, provided the model’s adaptation and timing mechanisms were also controlled.</p>

### 3.6 Memory organisation, sequencing and routing

<p>A proposed memory task would present several items in sequence, remove the input during a delay, and then require their identities and order to be recalled. Recurrent assemblies would provide a candidate storage mechanism, while slower modulation would organise local PING activity. Theta–gamma theories motivate testing whether successive population cycles can organise distinct items within a slower cycle [Lisman and Jensen (2013)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2013.03.007). The model would compare slow modulation alone, local gamma alone, their combination and tonic drive, with comparable training opportunities and controls for mean activity.</p>

<p>Measurements would include retention accuracy, order errors, interference between items and the number of reliably recoverable items as delay and sequence length increased. Shifting or disrupting selected cycles while preserving input spike counts would test whether timing contributes to recall. The analysis would allow representations to occur in bursts, following the working-memory observations of [Lundqvist and colleagues (2016)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2016.02.028), without assuming that each gamma cycle stores exactly one item.</p>

<p>If reliable retention were established, a later extension could test whether a contextual cue routes a stored item to different receivers. Measuring the representation before and after transmission would help separate failure to retain an item from failure to retrieve or communicate it.</p>

### 3.7 Synaptic plasticity and spike timing

<p>The plasticity proposals would address how reciprocal weights reach and remain within a regime supporting PING. An initial study would apply explicit spike-timing-dependent rules to controlled presynaptic and postsynaptic spike trains, varying their relative phase while preserving spike counts. This builds on the timing dependence demonstrated by [Bi and Poo (1998)](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.18-24-10464.1998). Rules would be chosen separately for excitatory and inhibitory pathways, with their biological assumptions stated explicitly.</p>

<p>The next step would allow selected synapses to adapt within the recurrent circuit, combining timing-dependent modification with activity regulation. Inhibitory plasticity can regulate excitatory–inhibitory balance [Vogels and colleagues (2011)](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1211095), but whether a chosen rule also preserves a useful PING regime would be tested. Measurements would track E→I and I→E weight distributions, firing rates, rhythmicity and task performance as learning proceeded. Frozen checkpoints evaluated on new inputs would separate changes stored in the weights from effects requiring ongoing plasticity.</p>

<p>These simulations could identify conditions under which learning stabilises, weakens or eliminates rhythmic activity, and whether those changes help computation. They would connect the manually selected coupling used in the completed work to a prospective account of circuit development and adaptation.</p>
