## Goal

The full lifecycle remains a proposed reduced-model study. [Exp154](/exp154)
implements capacity and consumption-only lifetime tests, plus one fixed abstract
genome, internally driven prenatal development, juvenile food learning and slower
adult learning. Its animation replays the phases, native neural activity, choices,
energy, deaths and births; the accompanying plot tracks prediction error and pathway
weight norms. Evolution, viable weight-space mapping and genetic–environmental
comparisons remain proposed. The current demonstration uses one seed and one fixed
cue–energy mapping; the broader comparisons below are future work.

Investigate how evolution, development and experience jointly shape
excitatory-to-inhibitory (E→I) and inhibitory-to-excitatory (I→E) synaptic
strengths in a simple model organism receiving repeated object exposures.
Identify how the region of weights compatible with stable, effective behaviour
changes across the lifecycle, and how inherited rules and individual experience
guide trajectories through that region.

## Methods

1. **Model organism and environment.** Present a sequence of objects to a model
   organism that decides whether to consume or reject each object. Each object
   has one observable scalar sensory cue and a separate, hidden food energy
   change between −1 and +1 in normalised energy units, before metabolic costs.
   The cue predicts the energy change without directly revealing it. For the
   initial test, draw cues $x_t$ uniformly from $[0,1]$ and set

   $$
   \Delta E_t=s(2x_t-1),\qquad s\in\{-1,+1\}.
   $$

   Here $t$ indexes object exposures, $x_t$ is the observed scalar cue and
   $\Delta E_t$ is the hidden food energy change. The mapping sign $s$ is fixed
   within each lifetime; test both signs across lifetimes. Reveal the food energy
   change only through consumption; rejection reveals no outcome. Track an
   organism-level energy reserve outside the neural network:

   $$
   E_{t+1}=E_t-c+a_t\Delta E_t.
   $$

   $E_t$ is the reserve before exposure $t$, $c>0$ is the metabolic cost per
   exposure, and $a_t=1$ means consume while $a_t=0$ means reject. Start with a
   positive reserve $E_0$ and terminate the lifetime when it reaches zero or below.
   Choose the cost and initial budget so that rejecting everything cannot sustain
   the organism, while informed food selection can sustain it on average.
   Initially, the reserve is not an additional sensory input; a hunger signal is
   a possible later extension.

2. **Neural controller.** Use a small recurrent network containing excitatory and
   inhibitory rate neurons, initially around 16 excitatory and 4 inhibitory cells,
   to learn $V(x)$, the expected food energy change given observed scalar cue $x$.
   Consume when
   the prediction is positive, with occasional exploratory consumption. Fix
   sensory connections, the value readout and E→E/I→I weights within each lifetime;
   initially adapt only E→I and I→E strengths, with bounded, sign-preserving
   updates driven by prediction error after consumption. Gradients through the
   rate dynamics provide an initial engineering implementation, not a claim of
   biological plasticity. This makes adaptation of inhibitory feedback causally
   relevant rather than allowing a trainable readout to solve the task alone.

   [Inhibitory homeostatic plasticity](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1211095)
   is a related modelling precedent; the proposed gradient-based learner does
   not establish that biological mechanism.

3. **Inheritance and development.** Represent the genome as inherited parameters
   controlling initial connectivity, synaptic strength distributions, plasticity
   and maturation timing. This is an abstract genetic representation rather than
   a simulation of specific genes. Simulate a prenatal analogue dominated by
   internally generated activity, postnatal development incorporating sensory
   experience, and adulthood with continued learning.

   Exp154 now implements a reduced version: restore inherited weights at conception,
   run 12 internal-drive windows minimizing per-cell activity error around 0.25,
   then learn from consumed food for 20 juvenile encounters before reducing the
   adult learning rate. Only E→I and I→E weights adapt. Each birth starts a fresh
   food optimizer and energy reserve; acquired weights are not inherited.

4. **Evolution across generations.** Evaluate each genotype across multiple
   lifetimes and environments. Select and mutate inherited parameters according
   to energy acquisition, survival and neural activity costs, without prescribing
   a preferred E/I ratio. Offspring develop afresh without inheriting acquired
   synaptic weights.

5. **Lifecycle weight-space mapping.** At developmental checkpoints,
   systematically vary E→I and I→E coupling strengths to identify regions
   supporting stable activity and stage-appropriate function. Distinguish
   immediate tolerance with plasticity frozen from recovery with plasticity
   active, and overlay naturally developed trajectories. Measure per-neuron
   excitatory and inhibitory inputs, their cancellation, prediction accuracy and
   firing stability: the E→I/I→E weight ratio is not itself E/I balance. Show the
   changing regions and trajectories in a lifecycle plot with phases marked.
   Define viability criteria before execution; sampled weight distributions are
   not themselves bounds on viable weights.

6. **Genetic and environmental comparisons.** Cross genotypes, experience
   histories and developmental random seeds to estimate their contributions and
   interactions. Include controls freezing plasticity in either pathway or
   disabling developmental activity. Restricted plasticity tests whether E/I
   adaptation can support learning; later allow other connections to learn to
   test whether evolution favours this solution when alternatives are available.
   Interpret the resulting contributions within the model and its sampled
   environments, rather than as a universal genetic–environmental percentage.

## Expanded validation still proposed

The implemented single-mapping demonstration does not establish performance
across conditions. Before adding evolution, test whether the restricted
E/I controller can represent and learn both cue–energy mappings. Run separate
lifetimes for each mapping sign with multiple initialisations, comparing the
learning controller against the same network with plasticity disabled and an
oracle that consumes only positive-energy objects. Match object sequences,
initial energy and metabolic cost across comparisons. Log prediction error,
energy, firing activity and pathway strengths. Evaluate prediction error across
all presented cues using outcomes available to the simulator, while exposing
outcomes to the learner only after consumption. Require improved performance
across both mappings without unstable neural activity before making broader
learning claims; failure first calls for checking representational capacity and
the learning rule.
