Abstract#

We asked whether a PING classifier trained on separate digits could operate on a continuous MNIST stream without retraining. We preserved hidden state across digit boundaries while varying duration and input rate, with a segment-matched decoder.

Classification became informative when the stream supplied sufficient input over time, with losses under brief or weak presentations. This deprecated study motivated later variable-rate training and spike-count readout; it did not test blind boundary detection.

Results#

Variable-duration digit stream#

The label-to-prediction pairs were 5→0, 3→3, 4→4, 1→1, 7→8, giving 3 of 5 correct segments. The 200 ms, 10 Hz error occurred at a condition with 87.3% mean accuracy, so this single error does not establish an encoding-rate failure floor (Fig. 1).

Duration-rate accuracy#

Accuracy remained at the empty-input floor through 0.5 Hz, became informative by 2 Hz, and reached 79.1% at 5 Hz (Fig. 2).

Methods#

Reused seed-level measurements and illustrative figures described continuous digit classification with frozen PING weights. The documented protocol below specifies the evaluation and decoder; missing raw recordings prevent prediction replay and independent confirmation of historical model identities.

  1. Select classifiers and digits. MNIST handwritten digits [1] were sampled from the official test partition, separately from training and validation. The protocol selected the best validation epoch from each of 3 independently trained networks (seeds 42, 43, 44), each with 1024 excitatory (E) and 256 inhibitory (I) neurons, trained on 200 ms presentations at 25 Hz. Illustrative streams used seed 42 and distinct digit classes; population estimates used all seeds.

  2. Encode and concatenate. Independent Bernoulli draws at each Δ𝑡sim=0.1 ms timestep approximated Poisson input across 784 pixel channels; firing probability was pixel intensity times encoding rate times Δ𝑡sim1000. Encoding rates referred to full-intensity pixels, with lower intensities scaling them proportionally. Digits were concatenated with no gaps; rate and duration changed at known boundaries while hidden state continued without resetting within the stream.

  3. Vary evidence. Crossed presentation durations and encoding rates defined 48 conditions, each evaluated using 40 streams of 10 digits per seed. Lower-rate evaluations used 10 streams of 10 digits per seed at fixed 200 ms; higher-rate points reused that duration’s grid measurements. Conditions and decoder identities gives the complete grids and the separate constant-rate versus rate-compensated duration comparison.

  1. Integrate output evidence. E-neuron spikes drove a non-spiking leaky integrator with a one-timestep delay and zero initial state:

    𝑢out[𝑘]=𝛽out𝑢out[𝑘−1]+1−𝛽outΔ𝑡sim𝒔𝐸[𝑘−1]𝑊out.
    (1)

    Here 𝑘 indexes timesteps from zero, uout is the dimensionless class-state vector, sE the dimensionless E-spike vector, Wout the trained output weights, and βout the leak factor. The decoder used the trained output time constant τout, defaulting to 2 ms when unspecified; its historical value was not independently confirmed.

  1. Read and score segments. A trailing mean used the current presentation duration Tpresent, with w timesteps and wk available during startup:

    𝑧[𝑘]=1𝑤𝑘∑𝑗=𝑘−𝑤𝑘+1𝑘𝑢out[𝑗],𝑤𝑘=min(𝑤,𝑘+1).
    (2)

    Here 𝑗 indexes the window and 𝑧[𝑘] is the class-score vector. At each known segment endpoint the predicted class 𝑦̂ was

    𝑦̂[𝑘]=argmax𝑐𝑝class(𝑐,𝑘).
    (3)

    Here 𝑐 indexes classes and 𝑝class is softmax-normalized evidence, not calibrated confidence; quantitative sweeps selected scores directly. Readout duration matched presentation duration rather than varying independently, unlike the whole-trial average used in training. Correct predictions were counted per seed; captions report across-seed means and sample standard errors.

Appendix: Conditions and decoder identities#

The grid crossed durations 10 ms, 15 ms, 25 ms, 40 ms, 50 ms, 75 ms, 100 ms, 200 ms with encoding rates 5 Hz, 10 Hz, 25 Hz, 50 Hz, 100 Hz, 200 Hz per channel. Each duration–rate condition contained 1200 classified presentations across 3 seeds. The low-rate extension used 0.01 Hz, 0.025 Hz, 0.05 Hz, 0.1 Hz, 0.25 Hz, 0.5 Hz, 1 Hz, 2 Hz, 3 Hz with presentation and readout fixed at 200 ms.

The separate duration comparison used 25 ms, 50 ms, 100 ms, 200 ms, with 20 streams of 10 digits per seed. Constant encoding remained at 25 Hz; the compensated rate multiplied this by 200 ms divided by the current duration. The fixed illustrative stream contained 5 digits at 50 ms and 25 Hz. The variable conditions appear in Figure 1.

For a constant-duration stream, the boundary time tk of segment k was

𝑡𝑗=𝑗𝑇present.
(4)

Here 𝑗 indexes segments from zero and 𝑡𝑗 is physical boundary time; varying-duration boundaries instead summed preceding durations. The output probabilities were

𝑝class(𝑐,𝑘)=softmax(𝑧[𝑘])𝑐.
(5)

Softmax converted the evidence vector into non-negative values summing to one. The fixed illustrative stream selected its softmax maximum; the varying stream and sweeps selected the maximum logit. Matched readout and presentation obeyed

𝑇readout=𝑇present.
(6)

Here Treadout and Tpresent are the respective durations in ms. The window length was

𝑤=𝑇presentΔ𝑡sim.
(7)

At intermediate times the trailing window could include preceding-segment activity; at the scored endpoint it covered the current segment. This duration-matched decoder used known boundaries, not inferred boundary detection. The output leak was

𝛽out=exp(−Δ𝑡sim/𝜏out).
(8)

The exponential factor retained part of the previous state at each timestep; τout set the evidence-decay timescale. Equation (1) applied from timestep one onward, with uout [0] = 0. The window average divided by its available length during startup, as stated in Equation (2).
