## Appendix A — Discrete dynamics and gradient calculations

<span id="appendix-a1" />**A1 — Discrete neuronal and synaptic updates.** These updates governed the spiking circuits in this study; their standard parameters are listed in Table A1. Let $k$ denote the number of completed updates. We updated conductances using Equation 2, first applying exponential decay and then adding the full weighted spike increments. Both recurrent pathways used spikes emitted during the preceding update; current external input entered the excitatory conductance before voltage integration. Consequently, newly emitted E or I spikes first affected the partner population in the following update.

For each neuron, the updated conductances defined the total conductance and effective equilibrium voltage:

$$
\begin{aligned}
g_{\text{tot}} [ k + 1 ] & = g_{L} + g_{E} [ k + 1 ] + g_{I} [ k + 1 ] , \\
V_{\infty} [ k + 1 ] & = \frac{g_{L} E_{L} + g_{E} [ k + 1 ] E_{E} + g_{I} [ k + 1 ] E_{I}}{g_{\text{tot}} [ k + 1 ]} .
\end{aligned}
\tag{16}
$$

Holding these conductances constant over the step, we calculated the trial voltage

$$
V^{\ast} [ k + 1 ] = \max \{ V_{\text{floor}} , V [ k ] + ( V_{\infty} [ k + 1 ] - V [ k ] ) [ 1 - \exp ( - \frac{\Delta t_{\text{sim}} g_{\text{tot}} [ k + 1 ]}{C_{m}} ) ] \} .
\tag{17}
$$

Here, $g_{\text{tot}}$ is in µS, and $V_{\infty}$ and $V^{\ast}$ are voltages in mV. The numerical lower bound $V_{\text{floor}} = - 200$ mV guarded against extreme negative values: any calculated voltage below this bound was replaced by −200 mV before spike evaluation. Membrane capacitance $C_{m}$, timestep $\Delta t_{\text{sim}}$, leak conductance $g_{L}$, synaptic conductances $g_{E} , g_{I}$, and reversal potentials $E_{L} , E_{E} , E_{I}$ retain their Methods definitions.

After calculating the trial voltage, we decremented the integer refractory counter by one, with a lower bound of zero. A neuron emitted a spike only when the decremented counter was zero and $V^{\ast} [ k + 1 ] \ge V_{\text{th}}$. Spiking or still-refractory neurons were assigned the reset voltage $V_{\text{reset}} = - 65$ mV; otherwise, the trial voltage was retained. Each spike reloaded the counter with $N_{\text{ref}} = \frac{\tau_{\text{ref}}}{\Delta t_{\text{sim}}}$, where $\tau_{\text{ref}}$ is the population-specific refractory duration and $N_{\text{ref}}$ was an integer at every tested timestep. Spike eligibility therefore resumed on the $N_{\text{ref}}$-th subsequent update. Conductances continued evolving during refractory holds.

<span id="appendix-a2" />**A2 — Output-state recurrence.** We updated each output unit after the hidden population, using excitatory spikes delivered during the same timestep. With the indexing of [Appendix A1](#appendix-a1), the pre-reset state, output spike and retained state were

$$
\begin{aligned}
\tilde{u}_{c} [ k + 1 ] & = \beta_{\text{out}} u_{c} [ k ] + \frac{1 - \beta_{\text{out}}}{h} \sum_{j} W_{j c}^{\text{out}} s_{j}^{E} [ k + 1 ] , \\
s_{c}^{\text{out}} [ k + 1 ] & = 𝟏 [ \tilde{u}_{c} [ k + 1 ] \ge \theta_{\text{out}} ] , \\
u_{c} [ k + 1 ] & = \tilde{u}_{c} [ k + 1 ] - \theta_{\text{out}} s_{c}^{\text{out}} [ k + 1 ] .
\end{aligned}
\tag{18}
$$

Here, $W_{j c}^{\text{out}}$ is the dimensionless weight from hidden excitatory neuron $j$ to output class $c$, and $s_{j}^{E} [ k + 1 ]$ denotes its delivered spike. The decay factor was $\beta_{\text{out}} = \exp ( - \frac{\Delta t_{\text{sim}}}{\tau_{\text{out}}} )$, where $\tau_{\text{out}}$ is the output decay time. The dimensionless quantity $h = \frac{\Delta t_{\text{sim}}}{1 \, \text{ms}}$ expresses the timestep numerically in milliseconds and specifies the input-scaling convention. The indicator $𝟏 [ \cdot ]$ equals one when its condition holds and zero otherwise; $\theta_{\text{out}}$ is the output threshold. Only one threshold was subtracted per update, so the retained state could remain above threshold after a spike.

Both readout modes used these spiking output units and the same subtractive reset; they differed in the quantity accumulated for classification. TR01–TR05 mean-state readout accumulated the pre-reset state for Figs. [3](#loc-8)–[9](#loc-30), whereas TR06 spike-count readout accumulated the emitted spike for [Fig. 10](#loc-17):

$$
\begin{aligned}
M_{c} [ k + 1 ] & = M_{c} [ k ] + \tilde{u}_{c} [ k + 1 ] , \\
n_{c} [ k + 1 ] & = n_{c} [ k ] + s_{c}^{\text{out}} [ k + 1 ] .
\end{aligned}
\tag{19}
$$

Here, $M_{c}$ is the cumulative pre-reset state and $n_{c}$ the cumulative output-spike count. Neither accumulator decayed. The final logits were $\frac{M_{c} [ N_{t} ]}{N_{t}}$ or $n_{c} [ N_{t} ]$, respectively, where $N_{t}$ is the presentation’s step count. Output state and accumulators began at zero; supplied image-boundary resets cleared them before that step’s output drive was applied.

<span id="appendix-a3" />**A3 — Surrogate and damped derivatives.** These derivatives governed TR01–TR06 classifier training underlying Figs. [3](#loc-8)–[10](#loc-17). Spikes used the binary forward threshold and surrogate backward derivative in Equation 11. To implement voltage-gradient damping, we used a stop-gradient operator $\operatorname{sg} ( x )$, which returned $x$ but contributed zero derivative during backpropagation. We defined

$$
\mathcal{D}_{\alpha_{\text{grad}}} ( x ) = \alpha_{\text{grad}} x + ( 1 - \alpha_{\text{grad}} ) \operatorname{sg} ( x ) , \quad \alpha_{\text{grad}} = \frac{1}{d_{\text{grad}}} ,
\tag{20}
$$

where $d_{\text{grad}}$ is the damping divisor. We used $d_{\text{grad}} = 1 , 000$ in both loop-on and loop-off. This operation preserved the forward value while multiplying its backward derivative by $\alpha_{\text{grad}}$.

For both hidden populations, we applied this operation to the exponential-Euler membrane increment $F [ k ]$ from [Appendix A1](#appendix-a1), before voltage clipping and reset:

$$
\bar{V} [ k + 1 ] = V [ k ] + \mathcal{D}_{\alpha_{\text{grad}}} ( F [ k ] ) .
\tag{21}
$$

Here, $\bar{V}$ denotes voltage before clipping. Damping scaled every derivative through $F [ k ]$, including its dependence on conductances and previous voltage, while the additive $V [ k ]$ path retained unit derivative. At fixed updated conductances, the resulting backward rule was

$$
{( \frac{\partial \bar{V} [ k + 1 ]}{\partial V [ k ]} )}_{\text{BP}} = 1 - \alpha_{\text{grad}} [ 1 - \exp ( - \frac{\Delta t_{\text{sim}} g_{\text{tot}} [ k + 1 ]}{C_{m}} ) ] .
\tag{22}
$$

The subscript BP identifies the derivative used by backpropagation; timestep, total conductance and capacitance retain their [Appendix A1](#appendix-a1) definitions. Conductance recurrences and the surrogate spike function received no separate damping factor.

Hidden reset and refractory decisions used Boolean masks and integer counters that were not differentiated. The retained-voltage branch carried zero derivative when a neuron spiked or remained refractory, while the spike-output branch retained surrogate gradients whenever the refractory mask permitted spiking. Voltage clipping also blocked gradients below the numerical floor.

Output updates received no voltage-gradient damping. Their subtractive reset retained the derivative through the output spike, giving

$$
{( \frac{\partial u_{c} [ k + 1 ]}{\partial \tilde{u}_{c} [ k + 1 ]} )}_{\text{BP}} = 1 - \theta_{\text{out}} \psi ( \tilde{u}_{c} [ k + 1 ] - \theta_{\text{out}} ) ,
\tag{23}
$$

where $\psi$ denotes the surrogate derivative in Equation 11 and the output-state symbols retain their [Appendix A2](#appendix-a2) definitions. The current-step accumulator increment contributed a direct derivative for mean-state readout and a surrogate derivative for spike-count readout.

<span id="appendix-a4" />**A4 — Initialization transformation.** For each input or recurrent projection, we generated independent Gaussian draws and Bernoulli retention indicators, then formed the initial weights:

$$
\begin{aligned}
X_{j i} & \sim \mathcal{N} ( \mu_{\text{init}} , \sigma_{\text{init}}^{2} ) , \quad M_{j i} \sim \operatorname{Bernoulli} ( 1 - q_{\text{zero}} ) , \\
W_{j i} & = \frac{M_{j i} \max ( 0 , X_{j i} )}{( 1 - q_{\text{zero}} ) N_{\text{pre}}} .
\end{aligned}
\tag{24}
$$

Here, rows $j$ index presynaptic neurons, columns $i$ index postsynaptic neurons, and $N_{\text{pre}}$ is the presynaptic population size. The parent mean $\mu_{\text{init}}$ and standard deviation $\sigma_{\text{init}}$ follow the projection-specific parameters in Table A1; $X_{j i}$ and $W_{j i}$ are conductance increments in µS. Input connections used $q_{\text{zero}} = 0.95$, giving a retention-compensation factor of 20; recurrent connections used $q_{\text{zero}} = 0$. Negative Gaussian draws produced additional zeros through lower clamping.

Writing $\mu_{+} = \mathbb{E} [ \max ( 0 , X_{j i} ) ]$ for the mean after lower clamping, this transformation gave

$$
\mathbb{E} [ W_{j i} ] = \frac{\mu_{+}}{N_{\text{pre}}} , \quad \mathbb{E} [ \sum_{j = 1}^{N_{\text{pre}}} W_{j i} ] = \mu_{+} .
\tag{25}
$$

Thus, compensation for zeroing preserved the expected summed incoming weight after lower clamping. Parent parameters described the Gaussian before these transformations; realised matrix means and incoming sums additionally depended on finite sampling and Bernoulli zeroing. The retention mask was used only during initialization, so initially zero trainable weights could become positive during optimization.

Readout weights followed a separate initialization:

$$
Y_{j c} \sim \mathcal{N} ( \mu_{\text{out}} , \sigma_{\text{out}}^{2} ) , \quad W_{j c}^{\text{out}} = \max ( 0 , Y_{j c} ) .
\tag{26}
$$

Here, $c$ indexes output classes. These weights were dimensionless and used no additional Bernoulli zeroing or fan-in normalization. Their parent mean and standard deviation follow Table A1 for TR01–TR05 and the continuous-stream Methods for TR06.

## Appendix B — Measurement algorithms and boundary cases

<span id="appendix-b1" />**B1 — Spectral interpolation.** This refinement supplied the spectral frequencies in [Fig. 1](#loc-5)E,G, [Fig. 2](#loc-6)I and [Fig. 6](#loc-12)A. We refined the spectral maximum using a parabola through three neighbouring bins of linear power spectral density. Let $m$ index the largest-power bin within the search interval, choosing the lowest-frequency bin when maxima were tied. The interpolated spectral-peak frequency was

$$
\begin{aligned}
\delta_{m} & = \operatorname{clip}_{[ - \frac{1}{2} , \frac{1}{2} ]} [ \frac{S_{m - 1} - S_{m + 1}}{2 ( S_{m - 1} - 2 S_{m} + S_{m + 1} )} ] , \\
f_{\text{peak}} & = f_{m} + \delta_{m} \Delta f_{\text{bin}} ,
\end{aligned}
\tag{27}
$$

Here, $S_{j}$ is the power spectral density at frequency bin $j$, $f_{m}$ is the selected bin’s frequency, $\Delta f_{\text{bin}}$ is the frequency-bin spacing in hertz, and $\delta_{m}$ is a dimensionless offset. Clipping limits the displacement to half a bin.

Neighbouring bins could lie outside the search interval, and the interpolated frequency was not subsequently restricted to that interval.

<span id="appendix-b2" />**B2 — Rhythmicity metric implementation.** This algorithm supplied the rhythmicity metric values in [Fig. 2](#loc-6)C and [Fig. 5](#loc-10)C. We pooled excitatory spikes across neurons into complete 1-ms population-count bins, discarding any incomplete final bin. The dimensionless, uncentered autocorrelogram was

$$
A_{\text{corr}} [ \ell ] = \frac{\sum_{b = 0}^{N_{\text{bin}} - \ell - 1} n_{E} [ b ] n_{E} [ b + \ell ]}{( N_{\text{bin}} - \ell ) \bar{n}_{E}^{2}} , \quad \ell = 1 , \ldots , 100 .
\tag{28}
$$

Here, $n_{E} [ b ]$ is the excitatory population spike count in bin $b$, $N_{\text{bin}}$ is the number of complete bins, $\bar{n}_{E}$ is their mean count, and $\ell$ is the lag in milliseconds.

We computed the numerator by zero-padding the count sequence to the smallest power-of-two length of at least $2 N_{\text{bin}}$, then inverse-transforming the squared magnitude of its fast Fourier transform. The padding prevented circular wraparound. Division by $N_{\text{bin}} - \ell$ corrected for the decreasing number of overlapping bin pairs at longer lags, while division by $\bar{n}_{E}^{2}$ removed the overall firing-rate scale. Approximately independent stationary counts therefore had a baseline near one.

We excluded zero lag because it was dominated by spikes matching themselves. Before smoothing, we replaced this entry with its 1-ms neighbour and convolved the autocorrelogram with weights $( 0.25 , 0.5 , 0.25 )$, using zero padding beyond both ends. This reduced small finite-sample fluctuations during feature selection.

Denoting the smoothed values by $\tilde{A}_{\ell}$, we selected the first lag $\ell = 2 , \ldots , 99$ satisfying $\tilde{A}_{\ell} \le \tilde{A}_{\ell - 1}$ and $\tilde{A}_{\ell} < \tilde{A}_{\ell + 1}$. The lobe was the maximum at positive lags preceding this trough, with ties resolved by the earliest lag. These values entered the rhythmicity metric equation in Methods.

<span id="appendix-b3" />**B3 — Burst detection and cycle-boundary discretization.** This procedure supplied the TR03 equal-network neuron–cycle distributions in [Fig. 6](#loc-12)C–H. For each presentation, we summed inhibitory spikes across neurons at each 0.1-ms simulation step and convolved the resulting trace with a unit-sum Gaussian kernel of standard deviation 1 ms. The kernel extended ±4 ms (81 samples), with zero padding beyond the presentation boundaries. Candidate bursts were interior local maxima reaching at least 5% of the maximum smoothed value within that presentation. Flat maxima were represented by their middle sample, rounded down for even-length plateaus. Minimum peak separation was half the period corresponding to the network’s spectral-peak frequency $f_{\text{peak}}$, converted to simulation steps and rounded down, with a minimum of one step. Smaller competing peaks were removed first. The same network-level frequency set this separation for every presentation.

For consecutive detected peak indices $p_{i}$ and $p_{i + 1}$, we placed the intervening boundary at

$$
b_{i} = \lfloor \frac{p_{i} + p_{i + 1}}{2} \rfloor ,
\tag{29}
$$

where $b_{i}$ is an integer simulation-step index and the floor operation rounds downward. The outer boundaries were zero and $N_{t}$, the presentation’s number of simulation steps. Excitatory spikes were counted separately for each neuron in the resulting half-open intervals: the starting boundary was included and the ending boundary excluded. A spike at an internal boundary therefore belonged to the following interval. The first and last intervals extended to the presentation boundaries and could contain partial cycles. When only one burst was detected, its counting interval covered the entire presentation; presentations without detected bursts contributed no neuron–cycle pairs.

<span id="appendix-b4" />**B4 — Illustrative-example selection.**

For [Fig. 3](#loc-8)A–B, illustrative rasters used the seed-42, epoch-50 TR01 full-MNIST checkpoint from each architecture and the first digit-0 image in the official test set, presented for 200 ms.

For [Fig. 4](#loc-9), illustrative rasters used the unpenalised seed-42 TR02 loop-off classifier and the first image in the official test set, a digit 7, at $s = 0$ and $s = 1$. Both presentations lasted 200 ms. Fixed subsets of 200 E and 64 I neurons were selected uniformly without replacement using seed 0 for display.

For [Fig. 8](#loc-15), illustrative rasters used the unpenalised seed-42 TR02 loop-on classifier and official test image 0, a digit 7, at $\sigma = 14$ ms.

For the illustrative stream ([Fig. 10](#loc-17)A–D), we used the seed-42 TR06 classifier with five predefined presentation-duration–maximum-pixel-rate pairs: (100 ms, 5 Hz), (200 ms, 7.5 Hz), (50 ms, 25 Hz), (100 ms, 15 Hz) and (200 ms, 10 Hz). Each candidate contained five distinct digit classes drawn in random order, with one image sampled uniformly from each class in the official MNIST test partition.

Candidates were evaluated in a predetermined order, and the first yielding five correct classifications was retained for the illustration. The first candidate met this criterion, producing the sequence 1, 7, 9, 5 and 2. Its image-selection and spike-encoding seeds were 820000 and 830000, respectively. This illustrative selection was separate from the quantitative streaming evaluation. Rasters displayed the first 200 excitatory and 64 inhibitory neurons.

<span id="appendix-b5" />**B5 — Random-stream implementation.** These streams supplied the TR02–TR05 post-training comparisons in Figs. [3](#loc-8)–[9](#loc-30) and the TR06 continuous-stream evaluation in [Fig. 10](#loc-17). Post-training evaluation restarted a dedicated CPU encoding generator with seed 20260415, advancing it through successive batches of 64 images without reseeding between batches. Input spike trains were matched across networks when images, ordering, batch grouping, timestep, presentation duration and encoding rates agreed. Deletion and insertion used a separate generator with seed 20260416. Inhibitory replay shifts also used separate generators, with seeds determined by training replicate, jitter type and magnitude. Perturbation draws therefore did not advance the input-encoding stream.

Quantitative continuous-stream evaluation sampled images from the full official test partition rather than the fixed post-training evaluation subset.

One NumPy image-sampling generator, initialized with seed 82000, advanced through 40 streams, selecting five distinct image indices uniformly without replacement within each stream. Images could recur between streams; neither digit classes nor the complete bank were stratified. We used the same ordered indices for all networks and conditions. We recorded the sampled indices and labels and checked every condition’s labels against this bank.

For spike encoding, we used an integer-pairing map to assign a distinct seed to each network, duration, rate and stream:

$$
\begin{aligned}
C ( a , b ) & = \frac{( a + b ) ( a + b + 1 )}{2} + b , \\
\eta_{\text{encode}} & = 830000 + C ( C ( C ( i_{s} , i_{d} ) , i_{r} ) , j ) .
\end{aligned}
\tag{30}
$$

Here, $C$ maps two non-negative integers $a , b$ to one integer; $i_{s} \in \{ 0 , 1 , 2 \}$ indexes training seeds 42–44, $i_{d} \in \{ 0 , \ldots , 3 \}$ indexes durations 25, 50, 100 and 200 ms, $i_{r} \in \{ 0 , \ldots , 10 \}$ indexes the eleven input rates in ascending order, and $j \in \{ 0 , \ldots , 39 \}$ indexes streams. The seed $\eta_{\text{encode}}$ is unique within the 5,280 quantitative network–condition–stream combinations. A dedicated CPU PyTorch generator was initialized for each combination and advanced across its five presentations. Image selection did not consume encoding draws. Matching images therefore did not match their realized spike trains across conditions or networks.

## Appendix C — Mean-field closure and numerical specification

**C1 — Gain function and population-model assumptions.** This closure defined the mean-field calculations in [Fig. 2](#loc-6)G–I. For each population $P \in \{ E , I \}$, denoting excitatory and inhibitory neurons, we used the stationary noisy-LIF gain:

$$
\Phi_{P} ( I ) = {[ \tau_{\text{ref} , P} + \tau_{m , P} \sqrt{\pi} \int_{a_{P}}^{b_{P}} e^{u^{2}} ( 1 + \text{erf} ( u ) ) \, \mathrm{d} u ]}^{- 1} ,
\tag{31}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mu_{V , P} & = E_{L} + \frac{I}{g_{L , P}} , \\
a_{P} & = \frac{V_{\text{reset}} - \mu_{V , P}}{\sigma_{V}} , \quad b_{P} = \frac{V_{\text{th}} - \mu_{V , P}}{\sigma_{V}} .
\end{aligned}
\tag{32}
$$

Here, $I$ is mean input current in nA; $g_{L , P}$ is leak conductance in µS; and $\mu_{V , P}$ is the corresponding effective mean voltage. The leak reversal, reset and threshold voltages are $E_{L}$, $V_{\text{reset}}$ and $V_{\text{th}}$, respectively, in mV. Membrane and refractory times, $\tau_{m , P}$ and $\tau_{\text{ref} , P}$, are in milliseconds, giving $\Phi_{P}$ in ${\text{ms}}^{- 1}$. The integration variable $u$ is dimensionless, and $\text{erf}$ is the error function. This expression gives the stationary firing rate of a LIF neuron driven by Gaussian white current noise (Eq. 21 in Brunel, 2000).\[[4](#ref-4)\]

To construct the population model, we approximated each synaptic current as $g ( E_{\text{rev}} - V_{m} ) \approx g ( E_{\text{rev}} - E_{L} )$, where $g$ is synaptic conductance, $E_{\text{rev}}$ its reversal potential and $V_{m}$ membrane voltage. This fixed the driving forces at rest and omitted conductance-dependent shunting. We replaced presynaptic spike trains by population rates while retaining exponential conductance decay. Each population rate then relaxed towards the steady-state rate predicted for its current input, with relaxation time $\tau_{r , P} = \tau_{m , P}$. This relaxation law was an additional modelling assumption. The effective noise scale $\sigma_{V} = 4$ mV was prescribed, rather than estimated from voltage recordings or calculated from population activity; the population equations themselves were deterministic.

<span id="appendix-c2" />**C2 — Numerical solution details.**

We eliminated the stationary conductances and inhibitory rate analytically, reducing equilibrium at each of 401 equally spaced currents to one scalar excitatory-rate self-consistency residual. Brent’s method solved this residual between zero and the refractory-limited maximum excitatory rate. Accepted roots had an absolute residual no greater than $10^{- 11} \, {\text{ms}}^{- 1}$.

We calculated the four-variable continuous-time Jacobian analytically. Among eigenvalues with positive imaginary part greater than $10^{- 8} \, {\text{ms}}^{- 1}$, we selected the pair with the largest real part. The first change from negative to nonnegative real part bracketed onset. Brent’s method refined the crossing, recomputing the scalar equilibrium and analytical Jacobian at each trial current. Transversality used a centred real-part difference at currents $10^{- 5}$ nA below and above the refined onset.

The upward amplitude sweep used 25 equally spaced currents from $I_{\text{ext}}^{\ast} - 0.1$ to $I_{\text{ext}}^{\ast} + 0.55$ nA, where $I_{\text{ext}}^{\ast}$ is the refined onset current. We initialized the lowest-current equilibrium with its excitatory rate increased by $10^{- 3} \, {\text{ms}}^{- 1}$, then carried each integration’s final state into the next, including the transition to the descending sweep. Amplitudes used 1,001 fixed samples spanning the final 500 ms of each integration, rather than the adaptive solver-output times.

For negative values of the dimensionless gain-integration variable $u$, we evaluated the scaled complementary error function $\text{erfcx} ( - u )$ to avoid cancellation. For nonnegative arguments, the exponent $u^{2}$ was capped at 700. This kept the gain evaluation finite across the continuation range.

**C3 — Criticality calculation.** This classification supplied the transition assessment in [Fig. 2](#loc-6)H.

We defined the hysteresis gap as the maximum absolute difference between upward and downward peak-to-peak excitatory-rate amplitudes at matched currents. For the amplitude-squared regression, we included all upward-sweep points satisfying $I_{\text{ext}} > I_{\text{ext}}^{\ast} + 10^{- 9}$ nA, where $I_{\text{ext}}^{\ast}$ is the refined onset current. Membership depended only on current, without an amplitude cutoff.

For each included point $j$, we defined excess current $x_{j} = I_{\text{ext} , j} - I_{\text{ext}}^{\ast}$ and squared amplitude $y_{j} = {( A_{\text{pp} , j}^{\uparrow} )}^{2}$, where the upward arrow identifies the ascending sweep. We fitted $y_{j} \approx m x_{j} + c$ by unweighted least squares, with slope $m$ and freely fitted intercept $c$. The coefficient of determination was

$$
R_{\text{fit}}^{2} = 1 - \frac{\sum_{j} {[ y_{j} - ( m x_{j} + c ) ]}^{2}}{\sum_{j} {( y_{j} - \bar{y} )}^{2}} ,
\tag{33}
$$

where $\bar{y}$ is the mean squared amplitude over the included points. The numerator is the residual sum of squares, and the denominator is the centred total sum of squares.

We classified the sampled onset as consistent with a supercritical transition only when the hysteresis gap was below $10^{- 4} \, {\text{ms}}^{- 1}$, $m > 0$, and $R_{\text{fit}}^{2} > 0.9$. All other outcomes were labelled “subcritical/inconclusive”. This was a numerical classification of finite-duration sweeps; no first Lyapunov coefficient was calculated, so narrower bistability or an unstable periodic orbit was not excluded.

## Appendix D — Exact replay transformations

**D1 — Quantization and boundary reflection.** These transformations defined the inhibitory replay perturbations in [Fig. 8](#loc-15).

We converted Gaussian timing offsets to integer simulation-step displacements:

$$
q = \operatorname{round}_{\text{even}} ( \frac{\sigma Z}{\Delta t_{\text{sim}}} ) , \quad Z \sim \mathcal{N} ( 0 , 1 ) .
\tag{34}
$$

Here, $q$ is the integer displacement, $Z$ is a standard-normal draw, and $\sigma$ and $\Delta t_{\text{sim}}$ are the proposed jitter standard deviation and simulation timestep, respectively, in milliseconds. Rounding selected the nearest integer, with halfway ties resolved towards the even integer. Independent-spike jitter used one draw per event; group jitter used one shared draw per presentation and original source window.

For an integer $z$, reflection into inclusive integer bounds $[ L , U ]$ was

$$
\mathcal{R}_{L , U} ( z ) = \begin{cases}
L & U = L \\
L + w - \vert ( ( z - L ) \bmod ( 2 w ) ) - w \vert & U > L
\end{cases} , \quad w = U - L ,
\tag{35}
$$

where $w$ is the interval span and modulo returns a nonnegative remainder. This mapping implements repeated reflection, including offsets spanning multiple boundary crossings.

For independent jitter, an event at original index $k$ received candidate index $\mathcal{R}_{0 , N_{t} - 1} ( k + q )$, where $N_{t}$ is the presentation’s number of simulation steps. For group jitter, let $k_{\text{min}}$ and $k_{\text{max}}$ be the earliest and latest original inhibitory-event indices within a source window, across all inhibitory neurons. We reflected the shared displacement into $[ - k_{\text{min}} , N_{t} - 1 - k_{\text{max}} ]$, then added it to every event in that group. Group membership remained defined by the original timeline. Zero jitter returned the original stream unchanged.

**D2 — Collision resolution.** Collision-resolved replay supplied both [Fig. 8](#loc-15) perturbation series.

After reflection, we resolved duplicate destinations separately for each presentation and inhibitory neuron. Original chronological order determined event priority. At each pass, the earliest original event among those targeting the same timestep retained that destination; the remaining events advanced through offsets $+ 1 , - 1 , + 2 , - 2 , \ldots$, measured from their respective reflected candidate indices. Out-of-range proposals were skipped. All destinations were checked again after each pass, allowing newly introduced collisions to be resolved under the same priority rule.

Resolution ended when every destination was in range and unique within its presentation and neuron. We reconstructed a binary replay stream while preserving each inhibitory neuron’s spike count in every presentation. Collision adjustments acted on individual events and could therefore change relative timing within a shifted group.
