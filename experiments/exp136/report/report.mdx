## Results

### With coupling

The fixed setting reproduced selective locking in both cue periods.
Across the measured cue periods, selected-pair PLV was 0.960 for S1 and
0.961 for S2, versus 0.165 and 0.048 for the other sender. Selected-pair drift
was below 0.03 cycles/s; the other sender drifted by 4–5 cycles/s.
Brief high PLV before cueing did not persist across the baseline. In earlier checks without sensory modulations, none of three
new-input trials passed every criterion: selected-pair PLV ranged from 0.819 to
0.978, with drift below 0.06 cycles/s. Selection persisted, but phase precision
was not reliable under the fixed thresholds.

### Without coupling

Without coupling, R stayed near 53 Hz while each cued sender reached about
55.5 Hz. Selected-pair PLV fell to 0.124 and 0.209, with drift of 2.96 and
2.42 cycles/s. Inputs and sender activity matched Figure 1 exactly; R was
unaffected by the cues.

### Cue bias without reliable transfer

The initial weak-signal test gave input $R^2$ scores of 0.597/0.410, sender
scores of 0.017/−0.144 and cued receiver scores of −0.062/−0.117; cue benefits
were −0.055/+0.095. After the search, fresh sender scores were 0.517/0.484,
receiver cue benefits averaged 0.322/0.318, and cued reconstruction was
0.085/−0.008 (Figure 3B). Scrambling raised cued reconstruction to 0.203/0.148
but reversed mean cue benefits to −0.091/−0.165. Thus an average cue bias did
not produce reliable reconstruction in both directions. These descriptive
scores from three pairs, one wiring and one linear readout cannot exclude
other codes.

## Methods

1. We used two sender networks, S1 and S2, and one receiver, R, with local
   settings inherited from [exp133](/exp133) — [*Lowet (2015): Coupled Rhythms*](/exp133).
   Each had 80 excitatory (E) cells and 20 inhibitory (I) cells. Separate groups of
   32 cue cells excited their own sender.
2. The synchrony reference ran continuously for 4 seconds per trial. The same
   cue input was sent first to S1, then to S2. All weights stayed fixed: sender-to-R
   connections were active from time zero in Figure 1 and zero throughout Figure 2.
   Background inputs were 33 Hz/channel for each sender and 35 Hz/channel for R.
   Coupling was 0.045 μS per pathway and cue strength was 0.06 μS. During each
   reference trial, only cue inputs changed;
   all other weights and inputs matched between the coupled and control trials.
3. We measured timing from repeated bursts of excitatory spikes. Panel D in
   Figures 1–2 shows
   phase-locking value (PLV), using a centred 200 ms window every 10 ms:

   $$
   \mathrm{PLV}_{SR}(t)
   = \left|\frac{1}{N_t}\sum_{k\in W_t}
   \exp\!\left(2\pi i[\phi_S(k)-\phi_R(k)]\right)\right|.
   $$

   Here, $S$ is sender S1 or S2 and $R$ is the receiver. The phases
   $\phi_S(k)$ and $\phi_R(k)$ are measured in cycles at time sample $k$.
   $W_t$ contains the samples with valid phases in the 200 ms window centred
   at time $t$, and $N_t$ is their number. The symbol $i$ is the imaginary unit;
   the outer bars take the magnitude, giving a value from 0 to 1. Only full
   windows with at least 90% valid timing were shown. Windows cross cue changes,
   smoothing transitions. Whole-period PLV and drift numbers exclude 100 ms
   after each change.
   Figures 1–2 show the input sequence used for selection.

4. The fixed setting came from an earlier coarse-to-fine search following
   [ter Wal and Tiesinga (2017)](https://doi.org/10.3389/fncom.2017.00006).
   The reference pair reused the selected weights and original input sequence;
   the earlier new-input checks were not repeated.
5. For the initial signal test, independent slow fluctuations changed each sender’s
   background input by at most 1.5 Hz/channel. Both signals were always present.
   Three training and three test input/noise sequences each appeared in both cue
   orders, with identical sensory spikes across orders. All neural weights stayed
   fixed. The original reference figures were reused, not rerun with these signals.
6. Fixed ridge decoders reconstructed each signal from recent E/I population
   spike counts. Training alone set scaling and weights; one decoder per condition
   and signal was used across both cues. The same readout also checked inputs and
   senders. For each test sequence and cue, combining both orders, we measured

   $$
   R^2 = 1 - \frac{\sum_{k\in\mathcal T}(s_k-\hat{s}_k)^2}
                    {\sum_{k\in\mathcal T}(s_k-\bar{s})^2}.
   $$

   Here $\mathcal T$ is the set of test samples, $k$ indexes those samples,
   $s_k$ is the signal value,
   $\hat{s}_k$ its prediction, and $\bar{s}$ its mean over that set. A score of
   one is exact reconstruction; zero matches the constant test mean. Cue benefit
   compares the same signal when cued and uncued. We required positive local and
   cued receiver scores, cue benefit in both directions, and improvement over
   zero coupling before calling the result selective routing.
7. The signal trials included zero coupling and an artificial timing control.
   For the latter, native spike replay replaced emitted sender E spikes with
   independently shuffled times within 50 ms blocks. Every cell’s count in every
   block was preserved. Scrambling changes both rhythmic timing and coincident
   input, so it does not isolate phase alone.

8. We started the new search at the Figure 1–2 setting. The coarse search added
   16 combinations of signal strength, smoothing time, coupling and cue strength;
   the fine search added 16 combinations within ±35% of the chosen coarse values,
   plus the centre again. Mean drives and wiring stayed fixed. Each setting used
   one decoder-training and one validation signal/noise pair, both in both cue
   orders. Ranking first required both sender scores to reach $R^2\geq0.1$,
   then preferred positive cued receiver scores in both directions, then
   maximised the smaller same-signal cue benefit. Validation was reused during refinement.
9. We tested the chosen setting with three fresh training and three fresh test
   pairs, again in both cue orders and all three coupling/timing conditions.
   Search and confirmation readouts used trailing 50 ms population rates at lags
   0, 25 and 50 ms to predict the signal 50 ms earlier. Training alone set
   scaling and coefficients. We excluded the first 200 ms of each cue period
   and averaged scores across test pairs, rather than treating time samples as
   independent trials. No neural weights were trained. A timing-based
   interpretation also required readable scrambled sender signals and better
   cued reconstruction and cue benefit with intact timing.
