## Introduction

A capacitor has two metal plates separated by an insulator. Charging removes electrons from one plate and adds them to the other. The plates acquire equal and opposite net charges; electrons travel through the external wires, never across the insulating gap in this ideal model.\[[1](#ref-1)\]

Capacitance $C$, measured in farads (F), relates the upper plate's positive charge $Q$, in coulombs (C), to the voltage difference $V_C$, in volts (V):

$$
Q=C V_C.
\tag{1}
$$

A battery at voltage $V_b$ charges the capacitor through resistance $R$, measured in ohms (Ω). Conventional current $I$, in amperes (A), changes the voltage over time $t$, in seconds (s):\[[2](#ref-2)\]

$$
\begin{aligned}
C\frac{dV_C}{dt}&=I,\\
I&=\begin{cases}
(V_b-V_C)/R & \text{Charge},\\
0 & \text{Hold},\\
-V_C/R & \text{Discharge}.
\end{cases}
\end{aligned}
\tag{2}
$$

Charging slows as $V_C$ approaches $V_b$. Hold disconnects the external path; Discharge joins the plates through the resistor. Electron drift runs opposite to conventional current. The time constant $\tau$, in seconds, sets the charging and discharging timescale:

$$
\tau=RC.
\tag{3}
$$

Choose a mode below, or adjust capacitance, resistance and battery voltage. Blue dots represent electrons. The dots exaggerate size and motion and represent groups of electrons. Changing a slider starts a fresh charge; switching modes preserves voltage. **Restart** returns to an uncharged capacitor. **Pause** freezes time. The model omits leakage and dielectric breakdown.

## A biophysical neuron

A neuron's membrane separates conducting fluids and acts as a capacitor. Here the moving charges are ions: sodium ($\mathrm{Na}^{+}$) entering the cell can raise its voltage, while potassium ($\mathrm{K}^{+}$) leaving can lower it. Voltage-dependent channels control these flows. The animation uses the classic Hodgkin–Huxley squid-axon membrane model.\[[3](#ref-3)\] To receive presynaptic spikes, we add an excitatory synapse; this addition supplies the input current $I_{\mathrm{syn}}$ in the voltage equation below.

The membrane voltage $V_m$ is the inside voltage relative to outside, in millivolts (mV). Membrane capacitance per area $C_m$ is in μF/cm²; inward synaptic current density $I_{\mathrm{syn}}$ and ionic current densities $I_{\mathrm{Na}}$, $I_{\mathrm{K}}$ and leak $I_L$ are in μA/cm². Time $t$ is in milliseconds (ms):

$$
C_m\frac{dV_m}{dt}=I_{\mathrm{syn}}-I_{\mathrm{Na}}-I_{\mathrm{K}}-I_L.
\tag{4}
$$

For each pathway $p\in\{\mathrm{Na},\mathrm{K},L\}$, conductance density $g_p$ is in mS/cm² and reversal potential $E_p$ is in mV:

$$
I_p=g_p(V_m-E_p).
\tag{5}
$$

These ionic currents are positive outwards; the current plot reverses their signs so upward means positive charge entering. The reversal potentials are $E_{\mathrm{Na}}=50$ mV, $E_{\mathrm{K}}=-77$ mV and $E_L=-54.3$ mV. Sodium activation $m$, sodium availability $h$ and potassium activation $n$ are dimensionless gates between zero and one:

$$
\begin{aligned}
g_{\mathrm{Na}}&=\bar g_{\mathrm{Na}}m^3h,\\
g_{\mathrm{K}}&=\bar g_{\mathrm{K}}n^4.
\end{aligned}
\tag{6}
$$

The available maximum conductance densities are $\bar g_{\mathrm{Na}}=120f_{\mathrm{Na}}$ and $\bar g_{\mathrm{K}}=36f_{\mathrm{K}}$ mS/cm². The channel sliders set the fractions $f_{\mathrm{Na}}$ and $f_{\mathrm{K}}$ from zero to one, both initially one. Leak conductance is fixed at $g_L=0.3$ mS/cm². Each gate $x\in\{m,h,n\}$ evolves as:

$$
\frac{dx}{dt}=\alpha_x(V_m)(1-x)-\beta_x(V_m)x.
\tag{7}
$$

The rates $\alpha_x$ and $\beta_x$, in ms⁻¹, drive the gate towards one and zero, respectively. With $u=V_m/(1\,\mathrm{mV})$ the dimensionless numerical voltage, the animation uses the classical squid-axon rates at 6.3 °C in the inside-minus-outside voltage convention:\[[3](#ref-3), [4](#ref-4)\]

$$
\begin{aligned}
\alpha_m(V_m)&=\frac{0.1(u+40)}{1-e^{-(u+40)/10}}\;\mathrm{ms}^{-1},\\
\beta_m(V_m)&=4e^{-(u+65)/18}\;\mathrm{ms}^{-1}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{8}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\alpha_h(V_m)&=0.07e^{-(u+65)/20}\;\mathrm{ms}^{-1},\\
\beta_h(V_m)&=\frac{1}{1+e^{-(u+35)/10}}\;\mathrm{ms}^{-1}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{9}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\alpha_n(V_m)&=\frac{0.01(u+55)}{1-e^{-(u+55)/10}}\;\mathrm{ms}^{-1},\\
\beta_n(V_m)&=0.125e^{-(u+65)/80}\;\mathrm{ms}^{-1}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{10}
$$

Here $e$ is the base of the natural exponential. The apparent divisions by zero have finite limits: $\alpha_m=1$ ms⁻¹ at −40 mV and $\alpha_n=0.1$ ms⁻¹ at −55 mV. Each fresh demonstration starts at −65 mV with $x=\alpha_x/(\alpha_x+\beta_x)$, then runs for 200 ms without synaptic input before the displayed timeline begins. Membrane capacitance initially equals $C_m=1$ μF/cm².

In a biological synapse, an arriving presynaptic spike causes transmitter release, opening synaptic channels. We add a standard conductance-based synapse with instantaneous opening and exponential decay, as described in *Neuronal Dynamics*, Section 3.1, Equations (3.1)–(3.2). This is an extension to the original Hodgkin–Huxley membrane model.\[[5](#ref-5)\] Its conductance density $g_{\mathrm{syn}}$ is in mS/cm², and we write its current as inward-positive (the opposite sign convention to that reference):

$$
I_{\mathrm{syn}}=g_{\mathrm{syn}}(E_{\mathrm{syn}}-V_m).
\tag{11}
$$

The synaptic reversal potential is $E_{\mathrm{syn}}=0$ mV. Each arrival adds the selected weight $w$, in mS/cm², to this conductance; between arrivals it decays:

$$
\frac{dg_{\mathrm{syn}}}{dt}
=-\frac{g_{\mathrm{syn}}}{\tau_{\mathrm{syn}}}
+w\sum_j\delta(t-t_j).
\tag{12}
$$

We choose a decay time $\tau_{\mathrm{syn}}=2$ ms and default weight $w=0.2$ mS/cm² for this demonstration. The Dirac impulse $\delta$ has unit area and units of ms⁻¹, so each arrival increases conductance by $w$; this is equivalent to the exponential conductance in the cited model. Arrival times are $t_j=(5+40j)$ ms for $j=0,1,2,\ldots$, following a schematic 4 ms journey from a source with prescribed spikes. These timings are also demonstration choices; transmitter release and the presynaptic neuron's dynamics are not simulated.

Voltage and the three gates are integrated with fourth-order Runge–Kutta steps of 0.01 ms. Synaptic conductance decays exponentially, evaluated at each step's start, midpoint and end. Action potentials emerge from the channel dynamics, without a firing threshold or voltage reset. Adjust capacitance, synaptic strength or the available channels to explore the response.
