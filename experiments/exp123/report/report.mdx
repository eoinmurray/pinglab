## Background

We imported the trained PING network from
[exp022](/exp022) — [*MNIST*](/exp022)
and kept its weights fixed. The [parameter table](#parameters)

## Results

### Inhibitory decay and network dynamics

We tested one trained network on 500 MNIST test images, 50 per digit, with
ten random spike encodings per image. We reused each encoding at 19 inhibitory
decay settings, $\tau_{\mathrm{GABA}}$, from 3 to 30 ms. Each presentation lasted
200 ms, with network state reset between presentations. All five figures use
this shared sweep of 95,000 presentations. Sampling seeds, encoding rates and
simulation settings are listed in the [parameter table](#parameters).
No uncertainty estimates are shown.

Longer inhibitory decay slowed the rhythm and reduced firing
([Figure 1](#fig-overview), A and C). From 3 to 30 ms decay, the spectral peak
fell from 100.2 to 17.7 Hz and excitatory firing fell from 26,589 to 5,377
spikes/s. The fraction of excitatory neurons active per accepted cycle stayed
between 20.9% and 24.5% (B). Spectral peaks can select harmonics; these results
come from one trained network.

### Evidence across time

Higher-frequency groups generally developed high correct-digit scores sooner
([Figure 2](#fig-time)). The pattern varied by image; image 115 (F) kept much
lower scores than most examples.

<RunFigure experiment="exp123" file="confidence_time.png" number={2} id="fig-time" alt="Nine panels show families of mean true-class softmax traces against elapsed time, coloured by mean inhibitory-volley frequency; higher frequencies are darker.">
  Each panel shows one example image. Each line shows the correct digit's
  mean softmax score across ten encodings at one decay setting. Darker colours
  mean higher I-volley frequency, averaged across accepted trials over the
  full 200 ms. Frequency is the reciprocal of the mean interval between
  inhibitory volleys. The colour scale is shared with Figure 3. Scores are
  sampled every 1 ms; they are not accuracy or calibrated probabilities.
</RunFigure>

The nine examples are the first selected image indices: 0, 7, 29, 51, 71,
115, 140, 161 and 164. For digit $c$, $z_c(t)$ counts its output spikes by time $t$:

$$
z_c(t) = \sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor t / \Delta t_{\mathrm{sim}} \rfloor} s_c[k]. \tag{1}
$$

Here, $t$ is elapsed time, $\Delta t_{\mathrm{sim}}$ is the timestep, $k$ indexes
simulation steps, and $s_c[k]$ is one when output neuron $c$ spikes,
otherwise zero. We converted these logits into scores using softmax:

$$
p_c(t) = \frac{\exp(z_c(t))}{\sum_{j=0}^{9} \exp(z_j(t))}. \tag{2}
$$

Here, $p_c(t)$ is the score for digit $c$, and $j$ ranges over all ten digits.
We applied softmax before averaging the correct digit's score across ten
encodings. All scores start at 0.1. The network was trained using mean voltage;
this spike-count readout was not retrained or calibrated.

### Evidence per completed volley

Several images reached high scores within a few completed volleys
([Figure 3](#fig-cycles)). The traces do not align exactly: early scores vary
with decay, image 115 remains distinct, and later counts use different trial
subsets. Score saturation can also make traces look aligned.

<RunFigure experiment="exp123" file="confidence_cycles.png" number={3} id="fig-cycles" alt="Nine panels show mean true-class softmax traces against completed inhibitory volley count, coloured by mean I-volley frequency with higher frequencies darker; traces stop below 80 percent draw coverage.">
  The same images, scores and colours as <a href="#fig-time">Figure 2</a>,
  plotted against completed inhibitory volleys. Dots mark the trough between
  successive volleys; lines join integer counts. Each score averages available
  accepted trials and requires at least eight of ten encodings. Later counts
  can therefore include different trials. Count zero is the initial score of
  0.1; the final volley is excluded if it has no following trough.
</RunFigure>

We detected inhibitory volleys after 1 ms Gaussian smoothing, requiring peaks
at least 2 ms apart and prominence of at least one spike or 20% of the maximum,
whichever was larger. Accepted sequences needed a complete interior cycle and
at least 50% mean inhibitory participation. A volley ended at the trough
between its peak and the next; we sampled the accumulated output score there.
We did not extend traces beyond the last bounded completion.

### Sustained correct decisions

Higher frequencies generally accompanied shorter decision times, while median
volley counts stayed near one or two over much of the range
([Figure 4](#fig-decisions)). Decisions before the first trough count as one;
this does not mean a whole volley was necessary. These plots include only
eligible successful trials. Frequency uses the full 200 ms, including activity
after the decision, so the association does not show that frequency alone
sets classification speed.

<RunFigure experiment="exp123" file="sustained_decisions.png" number={4} id="fig-decisions" alt="Two pooled density plots show sustained-correct decision time and completed-volley count on horizontal axes against I-volley frequency on a shared vertical axis, with median curves.">
  A: time to a lasting correct decision. B: the first completed-volley count
  at or after that decision. Both are plotted against each trial's I-volley
  frequency. Cells span 5 ms × 5 Hz in A and one volley × 5 Hz in B. Darker
  cells contain more trials on a logarithmic scale; white cells are empty.
  Black lines show medians within 5 Hz bands. From 95,000 presentations,
  A includes 84,237 trials and B includes 80,341, using the rules below.
</RunFigure>

A lasting correct decision is the first update after which the correct digit
stays the unique output-spike-count winner through 200 ms. Ties break stability.
This looks back over the full trial; it is not an online stopping rule.

Of 95,000 trials, 84,284 had a lasting correct decision. Of these, 84,237
also had an accepted frequency and appear in A. Panel B also requires a
decision no later than the final bounded completion and at
least eight of ten encodings with enough completed volleys at that count,
leaving 80,341 trials.

### Spike cost at 80% accuracy

Slower settings saved spikes at the cost of time ([Figure 5](#fig-tradeoffs)).
At 3 ms decay, reaching 80% accuracy took 38 ms and 5,135 mean network spikes.
At 30 ms decay, it took 144 ms and 3,706 spikes: 28% fewer spikes, but
3.8 times longer. Mean I-volley frequencies were about 107 and 15 Hz.

<RunFigure experiment="exp123" file="deadline_tradeoffs.png" number={5} id="fig-tradeoffs" alt="Time to first reach 80 percent accuracy versus mean network spikes consumed, showing 14 frontier settings connected by a black line, coloured from pale pink to black with matched frequency and inhibitory-decay scales.">
  Time and mean network spikes needed to first reach 80% accuracy, checked
  every 1 ms. Both measurements use all 500 images and ten encodings per image,
  including incorrect predictions. Spikes include excitatory, inhibitory and
  output neurons, but exclude input. Circles show 14 of the 19 decay settings:
  each offers a speed–cost choice that no other setting improves on both
  measures. Lines join measured points only. Darker colours mean higher mean
  I-volley frequency over 200 ms. The second colour bar shows the corresponding
  decay settings; its spacing follows their measured frequencies.
</RunFigure>

For each setting, predictions used the largest accumulated output spike count;
the lowest digit won ties. All 19 settings reached 80%.
Unlike Figure 4, accuracy could fall again after
crossing the target. The 80% target was chosen for this exploratory comparison,
and the curve was selected on the same test sample. Costs are averages, not
individual stopping costs or physical energy. Other accuracy targets can
give different curves.
