## Results

<span id="loc-3" />

### Continuous classification and accuracy

One frozen PING network correctly classified five successive digits with varying presentation durations and input rates ([Fig. 1](#loc-4)A–D). Hidden neuronal state continued between digits, while output state and counts reset at supplied boundaries. This example demonstrates capability, not reliability across arbitrary streams. We reused the first stream with five correct decisions from a predefined candidate sequence; the first candidate qualified.

At 25 Hz, increasing presentation duration from 25 to 200 ms raised mean accuracy from {accuracy2525} to {accuracy20025} ([Fig. 1](#loc-4)E–F). At 200 ms, increasing input rate from 0.5 to 25 Hz raised accuracy from {accuracy20005} to {accuracy20025}. These comparisons used the same ordered images; variation from spike encoding and the small, fixed image sample remains.

## Methods

1. **Input range and classifiers.** The filtered-input calibration in [exp080](/exp080) — [*Decoder Accuracy Improves with Input Rate*](/exp080) motivated the input-rate range. We reused three frozen networks, trained with seeds 42–44, from [exp022](/exp022) — [*MNIST*](/exp022). Each contained 1,024 excitatory neurons, 256 inhibitory neurons and ten output leaky integrate-and-fire neurons, with learned input-to-excitatory and excitatory-to-output projections and fixed recurrent weights. Evaluation held all weights fixed; the revised sampling protocol did not require retraining.

2. **Simulation and refractory periods.** We used a 0.1-ms integration timestep. Hidden excitatory and inhibitory neurons remained at reset for 1.2 and 0.6 ms after a spike, respectively, corresponding to 12 and six timesteps. Output neurons had no refractory period. The repeated evaluation specified these physical durations explicitly, preserving the refractory holds used by the earlier fixed-timestep measurements.

3. **Training and checkpoint selection.** Training used 6,300 optimization images and 700 validation images for 50 epochs. Maximum-pixel input rates were sampled from 0.5, 0.75, 1, 1.5, 2, 3, 5, 7.5, 10, 15 and 25 Hz. After each epoch, we evaluated the network on three independently spike-encoded versions of the validation images. Mean validation cross-entropy was

   $$
   L_{\text{CE}} = - \frac{1}{D N} \sum_{d = 1}^{D} \sum_{i = 1}^{N} \log p_{i , y_{i}}^{( d )} .
   \tag{1}
   $$

   Here $N = 700$ is the number of validation images, $D = 3$ is the number of encoding draws, $y_{i}$ is the true class of image $i$, and $p_{i , y_{i}}^{( d )}$ is its true-class softmax share for draw $d$, defined below. We selected the epoch with the lowest $L_{\text{CE}}$. If epochs tied, we chose the one with higher validation accuracy, then the earlier epoch.

4. **Evaluation streams.** We repeated the quantitative evaluation using one prespecified bank of 40 five-image streams from the official 10,000-image MNIST test partition. Image sampling used seed 82000. Within each stream, we sampled five images uniformly without replacement and without class stratification; labels could repeat and images could recur across streams. Every network, duration and input rate used the same ordered image indices, so both image identity and preceding-image order were paired across conditions. We tested all eleven training rates at 25, 50, 100 and 200 ms ([Fig. 1](#loc-4)E–F). Each duration–rate–network condition contained 40 five-digit streams, giving 200 decisions. The 132 network–duration–rate conditions therefore yielded 26,400 decisions, not 26,400 independently sampled images. Duration and rate were constant within each quantitative stream. Batches contained five streams with separate neuronal states.

5. **Earlier evaluation and pairing.** The earlier evaluation sampled images separately by condition. Its seed formula mapped 44 duration–rate combinations onto only 34 image-sampling seeds per network: ten pairs shared identical streams while the other conditions remained unpaired. The current quantitative results replace that mixed design with a common image bank. Image selection and encoding seeds both changed; differences from the earlier estimates cannot be attributed to image pairing alone. We reused the previously selected capability recording independently of this repeated grid evaluation.

6. **Input encoding.** Pixels generated independent Bernoulli spikes at 0.1-ms resolution. Spike probability was proportional to pixel intensity and the condition’s maximum-pixel input rate. Image selection and spike encoding used separate random generators. Each network–duration–rate–stream combination had a distinct encoding seed, giving 5,280 seeds across the quantitative grid. Each generator advanced across its stream’s five presentations. Pairing therefore matched images and their order, not realized input spike trains. Digits followed without gaps; segment labels give their durations and input rates ([Fig. 1](#loc-4)A and [Fig. 4](#loc-10)A).

7. **State and decision boundaries.** Each stream began with freshly initialized hidden and output states. Hidden neuronal and synaptic state then persisted between digits within that stream. Output-neuron state and spike counts reset at every supplied digit boundary. The readout accumulated evidence over the full presentation ([Fig. 1](#loc-4)B–D and [Fig. 4](#loc-10)B–D).

8. **Spike-count classification.** The cumulative score for class $c$ at timestep $k$ was

   <span id="eq-cumulative-count" />

   $$
   z_{c [ k ]} = \sum_{j = k_{0}}^{k} s_{c [ j ]} ,
   \tag{2}
   $$

   where $k_{0}$ is the first timestep of the presentation and $s_{c [ j ]}$ equals 1 when output neuron $c$ spikes at timestep $j$, and 0 otherwise. The displayed class share was

   <span id="eq-count-share" />

   $$
   p_{c [ k ]} = \frac{\exp ( z_{c [ k ]} )}{\sum_{a = 0}^{9} \exp ( z_{a [ k ]} )} ,
   \tag{3}
   $$

   where $a$ indexes the ten digit classes. These softmax shares are not calibrated probabilities.

   At the final timestep $k_{\text{end}}$, we predicted

   $$
   \hat{y} = \arg \max_{c \in \{ 0 , \ldots , 9 \}} z_{c [ k_{\text{end}} ]} = \arg \max_{c \in \{ 0 , \ldots , 9 \}} p_{c [ k_{\text{end}} ]} ,
   \tag{4}
   $$

   where $\hat{y}$ is the predicted digit: the class with the greatest final cumulative count, equivalently the largest final displayed share ([Fig. 2](#loc-6)C). Ties selected the lowest class index, including class 0 when all outputs were silent.

9. **Performance summaries.** We calculated accuracy for each duration–rate–network condition and summarized network-level measurements as means and SEM across three training replicates ([Fig. 1](#loc-4)E–F). Accuracy included silent presentations and all other decisions. SEM was sample SD divided by the square root of three; it summarizes observed variation across networks and their encoding draws, not uncertainty across independent image banks. The 200-ms curve reuses the corresponding heatmap evaluations. One shared image bank and one encoding draw per network, condition and stream do not separately estimate image-sampling, encoding and training variability.

10. **Capability example.** We reused a seed-42 recording selected with the predefined sequence of duration–rate pairs: {showcaseConditions}. We fixed candidate order and digit-sampling and encoding seeds before inference. [Fig. 1](#loc-4) uses the first candidate achieving five correct decisions. This search used five distinct digit classes per candidate, unlike the quantitative image sampling, which allowed repeated labels. The first candidate, with image-selection seed 820000 and encoding seed 830000, qualified. It was not drawn from the quantitative image bank.

11. **Failure example.** We reused a separately specified seed-42 stream with pairs (200 ms, 0.5 Hz), (50 ms, 25 Hz), (100 ms, 2 Hz), (25 ms, 10 Hz) and (200 ms, 5 Hz). This example was not selected through the capability search ([Fig. 4](#loc-10)).

12. **Readout close-up.** We selected the first correct digit from a separate 200-ms, 5-Hz stream ([Fig. 2](#loc-6)) and enlarged its 91.5–94.5-ms interval post hoc ([Fig. 3](#loc-8)). Equations [Equation 2](#eq-cumulative-count) and [Equation 3](#eq-count-share) define the displayed counts and shares.

13. **Scope of inference.** We evaluated classification with known boundaries and continuing hidden state, without testing autonomous segmentation, a hidden-state-reset control, or gamma activity’s causal contribution to recognition. Presentation and readout windows changed together, so the duration comparison does not separate extra sensory exposure from extra integration time. The selected success and separately specified failure stream are illustrations, not estimates of the frequency of such outcomes.
