## Abstract

Inspired by [Renart et al. (2010)](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1179850),
we simulated 4,000 excitatory and 1,000 inhibitory neurons receiving shared input.
Over {data.window_ms / 1000} seconds, neurons fired irregularly while their
collective activity stayed weakly correlated. Opposing excitatory and inhibitory
currents partly cancelled shared fluctuations. This supports an asynchronous
irregular (AI) state in this network. Other seeds and input conditions remain
untested, and the slowest neurons are poorly sampled.

### Verifying AI state

We check irregular firing, weak correlations, population averaging and opposing
current fluctuations. Together these support the AI interpretation; no single
panel is a pass/fail test.

We now check whether this network is in an asynchronous irregular (AI) state.
Following [Renart et al. (2010)](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1179850), we use
irregular firing, weak pairwise correlations, population averaging and current
cancellation as complementary checks. These metrics are adapted to our simulation;
together they assess the AI interpretation rather than provide a single pass/fail test.

**Panel A — Irregular firing.**

This plot shows how unevenly individual neurons space their spikes. Values near
one indicate irregular firing; values near zero indicate regular firing. Our
neurons mostly fire irregularly.

$$
\mathrm{CV}_i = \frac{\sigma(\mathrm{ISI}_i)}{\langle\mathrm{ISI}_i\rangle}.
$$

For neuron $i$, $\mathrm{ISI}_i$ contains the gaps between spikes; $\sigma$ is
their standard deviation and angle brackets give their mean.

We look for CV
near one rather than near zero, which would mean regular firing. Our medians
are {round(populations.E.median_cv, 2)} for E and
{round(populations.I.median_cv, 2)} for I, supporting irregular firing.

**Panel B — Weak average synchrony.**

This plot asks whether neurons tend to fire more or less at the same time. The
averages stay near zero, meaning little shared fluctuation on average, although
individual pairs can still be correlated.

$$
r_{ij}(\Delta)=\frac{\operatorname{Cov}_t(n_i^\Delta,n_j^\Delta)}
{\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}_t(n_i^\Delta)\operatorname{Var}_t(n_j^\Delta)}}.
$$

Here $n_i^\Delta$ and $n_j^\Delta$ count spikes from neurons $i,j$ in windows of
length $\Delta$. Covariance and variance measure joint and individual fluctuations
across windows $t$.

We look for mean correlations $r_{ij}$ near zero. All three
pair types stay close to zero, supporting weak average synchrony. Individual pairs
can still be correlated; the shaded spread includes noise from the short recording.
Windows advance by {data.correlation_step_ms} ms, following Renart's sliding-window
approach. Overlapping windows do not provide independent samples.

**Panel C — Fluctuations average away.**

This plot shows whether averaging more neurons makes population activity
steadier. Our curves fall close to the independent-neuron reference, suggesting
that individual fluctuations largely cancel rather than form a strong collective
fluctuation.

$$
Q(K)=\frac{\operatorname{Var}_t\left(K^{-1}\sum_{i\in G_K}n_i\right)}
{K^{-1}\sum_{i\in G_K}\operatorname{Var}_t(n_i)}.
$$

Here $G_K$ contains $K$ neurons and $n_i$ is neuron $i$'s count in each 20 ms bin.
The ratio $Q$ compares the variance of their average with their mean individual
variance, across bins $t$. Independent neurons give

$$
Q(K)=\frac{1}{K}.
$$

We look for a decline along this reference rather than a plateau. Our slopes
are {round(populations.E.scaling.loglog_slope, 2)} for E and
{round(populations.I.scaling.loglog_slope, 2)} for I, close to −1. Fluctuations
therefore largely average away. This compares groups within one network,
not networks of different sizes.

**Panel D — Opposing current fluctuations.**

This plot shows how excitatory and inhibitory current fluctuations counteract
each other. Their opposing contributions leave a smaller total fluctuation
shared between neurons, consistent with a mechanism that helps keep the network
asynchronous.

$$
C_{\mathrm{tot}}(\tau)=C_{EE}(\tau)+C_{II}(\tau)+C_{EI}(\tau)+C_{IE}(\tau),
$$

with

$$
C_{ab}(\tau)=\left\langle\operatorname{Cov}_t[I_i^a(t),I_j^b(t+\tau)]\right\rangle_{i\ne j}.
$$

Here $I_i^a(t)$ is signed current into neuron $i$ at time $t$; $a,b$ label
excitatory (E) or inhibitory (I) current, and $\tau$ is the time lag. Each $C$
is a covariance averaged over distinct sampled target pairs $i,j$, in nA²;
excitation includes external input.

We look for negative cross-type curves
opposing positive same-type curves, leaving a smaller black total. This cancels
{round(100 * (1 - cancellation.total_to_same_type_zero_lag), 1)}% of the
same-type covariance at zero lag.

### Comparison with the canonical network

Both networks use conductance-based LIF neurons. The canonical column describes
our [reference PING network](/notes/canonical-parameters); its COBA variant
switches off the E↔I loop. Values for this network come from the displayed run's saved
network and settings.

- **Size:** This network is roughly four times larger. Both networks have the same 4:1 E:I ratio.
- **Input:** This network drives both E and I directly with uniform Poisson input. Canonical PING drives E alone with pixel-dependent input.
- **Recurrence:** This network includes all four E/I pathways at 20% connectivity. Canonical PING has only the E↔I loop, with dense connections.
- **Response times:** This network has equal derived E/I time constants and equal synaptic decay times, with longer delays. Canonical PING has faster inhibitory neurons and faster excitation. Derived constants use capacitance divided by leak conductance, excluding synaptic conductance.
- **Weights:** This network uses physical conductance strengths; canonical weights use fan-in normalisation. Input rates alone therefore do not compare total drive.
