Abstract

Inspired by Renart et al. (2010), we simulated 4,000 excitatory and 1,000 inhibitory neurons receiving shared input. Over 30 seconds, neurons fired irregularly while their collective activity stayed weakly correlated. Opposing excitatory and inhibitory currents partly cancelled shared fluctuations. This supports an asynchronous irregular (AI) state in this network. Other seeds and input conditions remain untested, and the slowest neurons are poorly sampled.

Results

Raster

Each dot is a spike: excitatory (E) neurons are black and inhibitory (I) neurons are red. Their mean firing rates are 1.18 and 4.6 Hz. We measure 30 seconds after excluding the first 50 ms, allowing activity to settle.

Verifying AI state

We check irregular firing, weak correlations, population averaging and opposing current fluctuations. Together these support the AI interpretation; no single panel is a pass/fail test.

We now check whether this network is in an asynchronous irregular (AI) state. Following Renart et al. (2010), we use irregular firing, weak pairwise correlations, population averaging and current cancellation as complementary checks. These metrics are adapted to our simulation; together they assess the AI interpretation rather than provide a single pass/fail test.

Panel A — Irregular firing.

This plot shows how unevenly individual neurons space their spikes. Values near one indicate irregular firing; values near zero indicate regular firing. Our neurons mostly fire irregularly.

CVi=σ(ISIi)⟨ISIi⟩.\mathrm{CV}_i = \frac{\sigma(\mathrm{ISI}_i)}{\langle\mathrm{ISI}_i\rangle}.CVi​=⟨ISIi​⟩σ(ISIi​)​.

For neuron iii, ISIi\mathrm{ISI}_iISIi​ contains the gaps between spikes; σ\sigmaσ is their standard deviation and angle brackets give their mean.

We look for CV near one rather than near zero, which would mean regular firing. Our medians are 0.97 for E and 1.06 for I, supporting irregular firing.

Panel B — Weak average synchrony.

This plot asks whether neurons tend to fire more or less at the same time. The averages stay near zero, meaning little shared fluctuation on average, although individual pairs can still be correlated.

rij(Δ)=Cov⁡t(niΔ,njΔ)Var⁡t(niΔ)Var⁡t(njΔ).r_{ij}(\Delta)=\frac{\operatorname{Cov}_t(n_i^\Delta,n_j^\Delta)} {\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}_t(n_i^\Delta)\operatorname{Var}_t(n_j^\Delta)}}.rij​(Δ)=Vart​(niΔ​)Vart​(njΔ​)​Covt​(niΔ​,njΔ​)​.

Here niΔn_i^\DeltaniΔ​ and njΔn_j^\DeltanjΔ​ count spikes from neurons i,ji,ji,j in windows of length Δ\DeltaΔ. Covariance and variance measure joint and individual fluctuations across windows ttt.

We look for mean correlations rijr_{ij}rij​ near zero. All three pair types stay close to zero, supporting weak average synchrony. Individual pairs can still be correlated; the shaded spread includes noise from the short recording. Windows advance by 1 ms, following Renart’s sliding-window approach. Overlapping windows do not provide independent samples.

Panel C — Fluctuations average away.

This plot shows whether averaging more neurons makes population activity steadier. Our curves fall close to the independent-neuron reference, suggesting that individual fluctuations largely cancel rather than form a strong collective fluctuation.

Q(K)=Var⁡t(K−1∑i∈GKni)K−1∑i∈GKVar⁡t(ni).Q(K)=\frac{\operatorname{Var}_t\left(K^{-1}\sum_{i\in G_K}n_i\right)} {K^{-1}\sum_{i\in G_K}\operatorname{Var}_t(n_i)}.Q(K)=K−1∑i∈GK​​Vart​(ni​)Vart​(K−1∑i∈GK​​ni​)​.

Here GKG_KGK​ contains KKK neurons and nin_ini​ is neuron iii‘s count in each 20 ms bin. The ratio QQQ compares the variance of their average with their mean individual variance, across bins ttt. Independent neurons give

Q(K)=1K.Q(K)=\frac{1}{K}.Q(K)=K1​.

We look for a decline along this reference rather than a plateau. Our slopes are -0.94 for E and -1.05 for I, close to −1. Fluctuations therefore largely average away. This compares groups within one network, not networks of different sizes.

Panel D — Opposing current fluctuations.

This plot shows how excitatory and inhibitory current fluctuations counteract each other. Their opposing contributions leave a smaller total fluctuation shared between neurons, consistent with a mechanism that helps keep the network asynchronous.

Ctot(τ)=CEE(τ)+CII(τ)+CEI(τ)+CIE(τ),C_{\mathrm{tot}}(\tau)=C_{EE}(\tau)+C_{II}(\tau)+C_{EI}(\tau)+C_{IE}(\tau),Ctot​(τ)=CEE​(τ)+CII​(τ)+CEI​(τ)+CIE​(τ),

with

Cab(τ)=⟨Cov⁡t[Iia(t),Ijb(t+τ)]⟩i≠j.C_{ab}(\tau)=\left\langle\operatorname{Cov}_t[I_i^a(t),I_j^b(t+\tau)]\right\rangle_{i\ne j}.Cab​(τ)=⟨Covt​[Iia​(t),Ijb​(t+τ)]⟩i=j​.

Here Iia(t)I_i^a(t)Iia​(t) is signed current into neuron iii at time ttt; a,ba,ba,b label excitatory (E) or inhibitory (I) current, and τ\tauτ is the time lag. Each CCC is a covariance averaged over distinct sampled target pairs i,ji,ji,j, in nA²; excitation includes external input.

We look for negative cross-type curves opposing positive same-type curves, leaving a smaller black total. This cancels 71.2% of the same-type covariance at zero lag.

Both networks use conductance-based LIF neurons. The canonical column describes our reference PING network; its COBA variant switches off the E↔I loop. Values for this network come from the displayed run’s saved network and settings.

  • Size: This network is roughly four times larger. Both networks have the same 4:1 E:I ratio.
  • Input: This network drives both E and I directly with uniform Poisson input. Canonical PING drives E alone with pixel-dependent input.
  • Recurrence: This network includes all four E/I pathways at 20% connectivity. Canonical PING has only the E↔I loop, with dense connections.
  • Response times: This network has equal derived E/I time constants and equal synaptic decay times, with longer delays. Canonical PING has faster inhibitory neurons and faster excitation. Derived constants use capacitance divided by leak conductance, excluding synaptic conductance.
  • Weights: This network uses physical conductance strengths; canonical weights use fan-in normalisation. Input rates alone therefore do not compare total drive.

The following appendices document each panel of Figure 2. They adapt the diagnostics discussed by Renart et al. (2010) to this simulation. All panels use the 30-second recording after 50 ms burn-in. Cell selection, counting windows and plotting choices below describe this implementation, rather than an exact copy of the paper.

Appendix A — Panel A: irregular firing

  1. Select neurons and measure intervals.

    Use all recorded E and I spike trains after burn-in. Keep cells with at least 20 spikes. For each retained neuron, measure gaps between consecutive spikes; do not include gaps crossing the start or end of the measurement period.

    di,k=ti,k+1−ti,k,k=1,…,mi.(A1)d_{i,k}=t_{i,k+1}-t_{i,k},\qquad k=1,\ldots,m_i. \tag{A1}di,k​=ti,k+1​−ti,k​,k=1,…,mi​.(A1)

    Here iii identifies a neuron, ti,kt_{i,k}ti,k​ is its kkkth spike time in ms, di,kd_{i,k}di,k​ is an interspike interval, and mim_imi​ is its number of intervals (one fewer than its spike count).

  2. Calculate variability.

    Calculate each neuron’s mean interval and standard deviation, then divide them to obtain its coefficient of variation (CV). The implementation uses the population standard deviation, dividing by mim_imi​ rather than mi−1m_i-1mi​−1.

    μi=1mi∑k=1midi,k,σi=1mi∑k=1mi(di,k−μi)2,CVi=σiμi.(A2)\mu_i=\frac{1}{m_i}\sum_{k=1}^{m_i}d_{i,k},\qquad \sigma_i=\sqrt{\frac{1}{m_i}\sum_{k=1}^{m_i}(d_{i,k}-\mu_i)^2},\qquad \mathrm{CV}_i=\frac{\sigma_i}{\mu_i}. \tag{A2}μi​=mi​1​k=1∑mi​​di,k​,σi​=mi​1​k=1∑mi​​(di,k​−μi​)2​,CVi​=μi​σi​​.(A2)

    Here μi\mu_iμi​ and σi\sigma_iσi​ are in ms, so CV has no units. Cells without a finite CV are excluded from the histogram. No smoothing or fitted distribution is applied.

  3. Plot the distributions.

    Use 25 equal-width bins shared by E and I. They run from zero to the larger of 2.5 or 1.05 times the largest eligible CV. Plot each population as a separately normalised density histogram:

    Hp(b)=Np(b)Mph.(A3)H_p(b)=\frac{N_p(b)}{M_p h}. \tag{A3}Hp​(b)=Mp​hNp​(b)​.(A3)

    Here ppp denotes E or I, bbb identifies a histogram bin, Np(b)N_p(b)Np​(b) counts eligible neurons in that bin, MpM_pMp​ is the population’s total number of eligible neurons, and hhh is the bin width in CV units. Thus each histogram has area one. The x-axis is CV and the y-axis is density, not a neuron count. Black shows E and red shows I. Annotations give medians and eligibility counts; the vertical dashed line marks CV = 1. Values near zero indicate regular firing; values near one support irregular firing, without proving a Poisson process.

Appendix B — Panel B: weak average synchrony

  1. Choose cells and slide the counting window.

    Select up to 200 evenly spaced neuron indices from each population. This selection is fixed, rather than randomly redrawn. Count spikes in complete 5, 20 and 50 ms windows, each advanced by 1 ms. No window extends into burn-in or beyond the recording.

    niΔ(m)=∑k1 ⁣[mδ≤ti,k<mδ+Δ].(B1)n_i^{\Delta}(m)=\sum_k \mathbf{1}\!\left[m\delta\leq t_{i,k}<m\delta+\Delta\right]. \tag{B1}niΔ​(m)=k∑​1[mδ≤ti,k​<mδ+Δ].(B1)

    Here niΔ(m)n_i^{\Delta}(m)niΔ​(m) is neuron iii‘s count in window mmm, ti,kt_{i,k}ti,k​ is a post-burn-in spike time, Δ\DeltaΔ is the window width, δ\deltaδ is the slide step (all times in ms), and 1\mathbf{1}1 is one when its condition holds and zero otherwise. The sum runs over that neuron’s spikes. Windows start at mδm\deltamδ for nonnegative integers mmm satisfying mδ+Δ≤Tm\delta+\Delta\leq Tmδ+Δ≤T, where TTT is the measurement duration. The code bins at the slide step and uses cumulative sums to obtain these same counts efficiently.

  2. Calculate each pair’s correlation.

    For each window width, subtract each cell’s mean count across windows and calculate Pearson correlation. Exclude cells whose counts have zero variance at that width; the 20-spike threshold from Panel A does not apply here.

    xi(m)=niΔ(m)−nˉiΔ,rij(Δ)=∑mxi(m)xj(m)∑mxi(m)2∑mxj(m)2.(B2)x_i(m)=n_i^{\Delta}(m)-\bar n_i^{\Delta},\qquad r_{ij}(\Delta)=\frac{\sum_m x_i(m)x_j(m)} {\sqrt{\sum_m x_i(m)^2\sum_m x_j(m)^2}}. \tag{B2}xi​(m)=niΔ​(m)−nˉiΔ​,rij​(Δ)=∑m​xi​(m)2∑m​xj​(m)2​∑m​xi​(m)xj​(m)​.(B2)

    Here nˉiΔ\bar n_i^{\Delta}nˉiΔ​ is the mean count over complete windows, xi(m)x_i(m)xi​(m) is its centred count, i,ji,ji,j identify distinct neurons, and the sums cover the same windows for both cells. Correlation rijr_{ij}rij​ has no units and lies between −1 and +1. It measures same-window association, rather than a lagged correlation.

  3. Plot means and pairwise spread.

    Collect each distinct E–E and I–I pair once, and every selected E–I pair. For each pair type and window width, plot its mean correlation and shade one standard deviation on either side:

    rˉ=1P∑p=1Prp,sr=1P∑p=1P(rp−rˉ)2.(B3)\bar r=\frac{1}{P}\sum_{p=1}^{P}r_p,\qquad s_r=\sqrt{\frac{1}{P}\sum_{p=1}^{P}(r_p-\bar r)^2}. \tag{B3}rˉ=P1​p=1∑P​rp​,sr​=P1​p=1∑P​(rp​−rˉ)2​.(B3)

    Here PPP is the number of valid neuron pairs, rpr_prp​ is one pair’s correlation, rˉ\bar rrˉ is the plotted mean, and srs_rsr​ is the pairwise standard deviation. The x-axis is counting-window width in ms, not elapsed recording time. The y-axis is mean Pearson correlation. Black shows E–E, red I–I and cyan E–I; the horizontal dashed line marks zero. Connecting lines join the three measured widths. Shading is spread between pairs, not a confidence interval. Overlapping windows and pairs sharing neurons are not independent observations.

Appendix C — Panel C: population averaging

  1. Count spikes and choose groups.

    Count all E and I spikes in disjoint 20 ms bins, separately for each population. Unlike Panel B, these bins do not slide. Include low-rate and silent neurons. Test group sizes 4, 8, 16, and successive powers of two through 2,048, capped at the population size; also include the full population and remove duplicate sizes. For each size, draw 50 groups without replacement within each group. Different groups may overlap and need not be nested. Selection uses fixed random seeds, separately for E and I.

  2. Measure how much averaging reduces fluctuations.

    For each group, average its counts in each bin, then compare the variance of this average with the mean individual variance:

    nˉG(m)=1K∑i∈Gni(m),QG(K)=Var⁡m[nˉG(m)]K−1∑i∈GVar⁡m[ni(m)].(C1)\bar n_G(m)=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{i\in G}n_i(m),\qquad Q_G(K)=\frac{\operatorname{Var}_m[\bar n_G(m)]} {K^{-1}\sum_{i\in G}\operatorname{Var}_m[n_i(m)]}. \tag{C1}nˉG​(m)=K1​i∈G∑​ni​(m),QG​(K)=K−1∑i∈G​Varm​[ni​(m)]Varm​[nˉG​(m)]​.(C1)

    Here GGG is a selected group of KKK neurons, iii identifies a member, mmm indexes 20 ms bins, ni(m)n_i(m)ni​(m) is its spike count, and nˉG(m)\bar n_G(m)nˉG​(m) is the group average. Variance is calculated across bins, using division by the number of bins. The ratio QGQ_GQG​ has no units. Exclude groups whose denominator is zero. For uncorrelated count fluctuations, the reference is

    QG(K)=1K.(C2)Q_G(K)=\frac{1}{K}. \tag{C2}QG​(K)=K1​.(C2)

    This reference does not require Poisson firing. Positive shared fluctuations can keep the ratio above it and produce a plateau at large group sizes.

  3. Plot groups and fit the slope.

    The x-axis is group size KKK and the y-axis is normalised variance QQQ; both axes are logarithmic. Black shows E and red shows I. At each size, plot the median across valid groups and shade their 5th–95th percentiles. The grey dashed line is the independent-neuron reference. Fit a straight line to all finite, positive median points in log–log coordinates:

    log⁡Qmedian(K)≈a+blog⁡K.(C3)\log Q_{\mathrm{median}}(K)\approx a+b\log K. \tag{C3}logQmedian​(K)≈a+blogK.(C3)

    Here QmedianQ_{\mathrm{median}}Qmedian​ is the median group ratio, aaa is a fitted intercept, and bbb is the slope shown in the legend. Each group size receives equal weight; the reference slope is −1. Shading describes group selection, not uncertainty across time or network seeds. At full population size, every draw contains the same neurons. This is a pooling test within one network, not a network-size sweep.

Appendix D — Panel D: current covariance cancellation

  1. Reconstruct signed currents.

    Use the 32 evenly spaced E target neurons with recorded voltages and conductances. Combine recurrent and external excitation. At each raw simulation step, reconstruct currents using that step’s conductances and the preceding voltage, matching the simulator’s held-conductance update:

    IiE[q]=(giE[q]+giX[q])(EE−Vi[q−1]),IiI[q]=giI[q](EI−Vi[q−1]).(D1)I_i^E[q]=(g_i^E[q]+g_i^X[q])(E_E-V_i[q-1]),\qquad I_i^I[q]=g_i^I[q](E_I-V_i[q-1]). \tag{D1}IiE​[q]=(giE​[q]+giX​[q])(EE​−Vi​[q−1]),IiI​[q]=giI​[q](EI​−Vi​[q−1]).(D1)

    Here qqq indexes raw simulation steps, iii identifies a sampled target, giE,giX,giIg_i^E,g_i^X,g_i^IgiE​,giX​,giI​ are recurrent excitatory, external excitatory and inhibitory conductances in µS, EE,EIE_E,E_IEE​,EI​ are reversal potentials in mV, and ViV_iVi​ is membrane voltage in mV. Signed currents IiE,IiII_i^E,I_i^IIiE​,IiI​ are in nA. The first raw step uses the resting voltage; reconstruction precedes burn-in removal, so the first measured step uses the final burn-in voltage. Excitation is generally positive and inhibition negative in this voltage range.

  2. Average covariance over time and distinct target pairs.

    Remove burn-in and subtract each current trace’s mean over the entire remaining recording. At each lag, average products of these centred currents over every valid time sample and every ordered pair of different target neurons:

    δIia[n]=Iia[n]−1N∑u=0N−1Iia[u],\delta I_i^a[n]=I_i^a[n]-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{u=0}^{N-1}I_i^a[u],δIia​[n]=Iia​[n]−N1​u=0∑N−1​Iia​[u], Cab(ℓ dt)=∑i≠j∑n∈OℓδIia[n] δIjb[n+ℓ]M(M−1)(N−∣ℓ∣).(D2)C_{ab}(\ell\,\mathrm{d}t)= \frac{\displaystyle\sum_{i\ne j}\sum_{n\in\mathcal O_\ell} \delta I_i^a[n]\,\delta I_j^b[n+\ell]} {M(M-1)(N-|\ell|)}. \tag{D2}Cab​(ℓdt)=M(M−1)(N−∣ℓ∣)i=j∑​n∈Oℓ​∑​δIia​[n]δIjb​[n+ℓ]​.(D2)

    Here a,ba,ba,b denote E or I current, MMM is the number of sampled targets, NNN is the number of post-burn-in samples, n,un,un,u index those samples, ℓ\ellℓ is an integer lag, and dt\mathrm{d}tdt is the simulation timestep. The overlap set Oℓ\mathcal O_\ellOℓ​ contains indices for which both nnn and n+ℓn+\elln+ℓ are inside the recording. Thus positive lag compares the first current with a later value of the second. The denominator uses the actual overlap length; means remain global rather than being recalculated at each lag.

    With 32 targets there are 992 ordered distinct-cell pairs. Same-cell terms are excluded. An FFT computes these sums efficiently with padding to prevent circular wraparound; it does not smooth the curves. All components use the same pair set and normalisation.

  3. Sum components and calculate cancellation.

    Compute the four components and their sum, and separately check that the sum matches covariance calculated from the total current:

    Ctot(τ)=CEE(τ)+CII(τ)+CEI(τ)+CIE(τ).(D3)C_{\mathrm{tot}}(\tau)=C_{EE}(\tau)+C_{II}(\tau)+C_{EI}(\tau)+C_{IE}(\tau). \tag{D3}Ctot​(τ)=CEE​(τ)+CII​(τ)+CEI​(τ)+CIE​(τ).(D3)

    Here τ=ℓ dt\tau=\ell\,\mathrm{d}tτ=ℓdt is lag in ms and CtotC_{\mathrm{tot}}Ctot​ is the between-cell covariance of summed E and I current. The cancellation percentage at zero lag is

    100[1−Ctot(0)CEE(0)+CII(0)]%.(D4)100\left[1-\frac{C_{\mathrm{tot}}(0)}{C_{EE}(0)+C_{II}(0)}\right]\%. \tag{D4}100[1−CEE​(0)+CII​(0)Ctot​(0)​]%.(D4)

    This compares the total with the sum of same-type components; it is reported when that denominator is positive. The in-plot annotation gives the ratio inside the subtraction, rather than the percentage. This decomposition describes coordinated fluctuations, not just a balance of mean currents.

  4. Filter only the additional total-current trace.

    For the grey dotted trace, first apply a causal 20 ms exponential filter to each cell’s total current, then calculate its covariance using the same procedure:

    Ji[q]=IiE[q]+IiI[q],J~i[q]=αJ~i[q−1]+(1−α)Ji[q],α=e−dt/τf.(D5)J_i[q]=I_i^E[q]+I_i^I[q],\qquad \widetilde J_i[q]=\alpha\widetilde J_i[q-1]+(1-\alpha)J_i[q],\qquad \alpha=e^{-\mathrm{d}t/\tau_f}. \tag{D5}Ji​[q]=IiE​[q]+IiI​[q],Ji​[q]=αJi​[q−1]+(1−α)Ji​[q],α=e−dt/τf​.(D5)

    Here JiJ_iJi​ is total current, J~i\widetilde J_iJi​ is filtered total current, τf=20\tau_f=20τf​=20 ms is the filter time constant, and α\alphaα is its decay factor. Initialise the filter at zero before the first raw step and filter the full record before removing burn-in. This is covariance of filtered currents, not smoothing applied to the plotted covariance curve.

  5. Read the axes and traces.

    The x-axis is lag in ms, from −50 to +50, at the simulation timestep. The y-axis is mean between-cell covariance in nA², not correlation bounded by −1 and +1. Dashed black shows EE, solid red II, solid cyan EI and dashed amber IE. Solid black is their total; the grey dotted line shows the filtered total’s covariance. E and I label current types here: every target neuron is E.

    Negative cross-type components oppose positive same-type components, reducing the black total around zero lag. The main curves receive no extra smoothing or fitted shape. They look smooth because synaptic currents have finite decay times and covariance is averaged over time and many pairs. Neither the pairs nor neighbouring lag points are independent, and no confidence band is shown.
